§ II

Inter-Institution Correlation Network

Before forces are computed, the coupling topology of the system is established via a Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage correlation network built on the leverage feature \(\ell_t^{(i)} = X_t^{(i,0)}\) (loan-to-asset ratio, feature index 0).

2.1 — Sample Covariance

Centred sample covariance
\[ S = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_t - \bar\ell)(\ell_t - \bar\ell)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}, \qquad \bar\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \ell_t \]

2.2 — Ledoit-Wolf Analytical Shrinkage

The shrinkage target is the scaled identity — the minimum-assumption prior that all institutions are equally variant and uncorrelated:

Shrinkage target (scaled identity)
\[ T^* = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(S)}{N}\,I_N \]
Oracle Approximating shrinkage intensity
\[ \rho = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\rho_{\text{num}}}{\rho_{\text{den}}},\; 0,\; 1\right), \qquad \rho_{\text{num}} = \frac{1}{T^2}\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Var}(X_{ti}X_{tj}), \qquad \rho_{\text{den}} = \|S - T^*\|_F^2 \] Implemented as: \[ \rho_{\text{num}} = \frac{1}{T}\left(\frac{X_c^{\odot 2\top} X_c^{\odot 2}}{T} - S^{\odot 2}\right)_{\!\text{sum}}\!\Big/\,T, \quad X_c^{\odot 2} = X_c \odot X_c \text{ (elementwise square)} \]
Closed-form ρ — direct from leverage panel, no iteration
\[ \rho = \operatorname{clip}\!\left( \frac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^2 N^2}\,\bigl\|X_c^{\odot 2}\bigr\|_F^2 - \frac{1}{T N^2}\|S\|_F^2} {\displaystyle\|S\|_F^2 - \frac{[\operatorname{tr}(S)]^2}{N}},\; 0,\;1\right) \] Scalar interpretation: \(\rho \approx 0\) when \(T \gg N\) (data-rich regime — trust empirical covariance); \(\rho \to 1\) when \(T \ll N\) (data-starved — pull toward independence prior). No optimisation loop required: compute \(\|X_c^{\odot 2}\|_F^2\), \(\|S\|_F^2\), and \(\operatorname{tr}(S)\) directly from the panel.
Inputs needed: Centred leverage panel \(X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times N}\), sample covariance \(S\)
Shrunk covariance
\[ \hat\Sigma = (1-\rho)\,S + \rho\,T^* \]

2.3 — Correlation Matrix \(W\)

The network adjacency weights are partial correlations, symmetrised and unit-diagonal:

Normalisation to correlation
\[ W_{ij} = \frac{\hat\Sigma_{ij}}{\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{ii}\,\hat\Sigma_{jj}}}, \qquad W_{ii} = 1, \qquad W = \frac{W + W^\top}{2} \]

2.4 — Network Spectral Radius

For symmetric positive semi-definite \(W\), all eigenvalues are real and \(\lambda_{\max}(W) = \rho(W)\). As correlations increase, \(\lambda_{\max}\) approaches \(N\) — perfect synchrony — the maximum contagion amplification factor.

Spectral radius of the network
\[ \rho(W) = \lambda_{\max}(W) = \max_k |\lambda_k|, \qquad W v_k = \lambda_k v_k \] Computed via symmetric eigensolver (eigh).
Closed-form bounds — no eigensolver needed
\[ \bar{W}_{\text{off}} \leq \frac{\rho(W) - 1}{N-1} \leq W_{\text{max}} \] \[ \rho(W) \leq \sqrt{N\bigl(1+(N-1)\,W_{\text{max}}^2\bigr)} \quad\text{(Frobenius bound: }\|W\|_F = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(W^2)},\;W_{ii}=1\text{)} \] Quick scalar proxy (replaces full eigen-decomposition for early-warning threshold checks): \[ \tilde\rho = 1 + (N-1)\,\bar{W}_{\text{off}} \] where \(\bar{W}_{\text{off}} = \frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}W_{ij}\) is the mean off-diagonal correlation. When \(\tilde\rho > N/2\), the threshold for network-amplified contagion is breached without computing a single eigenvalue.

2.5 — Relationship to GravityEngine Coupling

The network spectral radius \(\rho(W)\) is the macro-level diagnostic; the pairwise energy coupling constant \(\gamma\) in \(\Phi_{\text{pair}}\) is the micro-level parameter. They connect conceptually as:

Coupling correspondence (heuristic — not a derived equality)
\[ \gamma \;\sim\; \frac{\rho(W)}{N} \cdot \gamma_{\text{scale}} \] Note: \(\gamma_{\text{scale}}\) is a dimensional proportionality constant that depends on the physical units of the state space and has no closed-form expression in the current model. This correspondence is a conceptual heuristic, not a derived identity — both \(\gamma\) (pairwise coupling constant) and \(\rho(W)\) (spectral radius of the empirical correlation network) measure systemic coupling, but through incommensurable objects. A reviewer should not treat this as a theorem.
High \(\rho(W)\) implies that pairwise erf-log interactions collectively produce deeper cooperative potential wells — the system becomes easier to destabilise coherently.
§ III

Potential Energy Landscape

2.1 — Radial Spring (Confinement)

A harmonic well anchoring the system to equilibrium \(\mu\):

Radial spring energy
\[ \Phi_{\text{rad}}(X) = \frac{\alpha}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \|x_i - \mu\|^2 \]
α = 0.10Radial spring constant; controls basin steepness

2.2 — Pairwise Interaction (erf-log Potential)

From \(F_i = -\nabla_{x_i}\Phi\): the erf term contributes \(+\frac{2\gamma}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-r^2/\sigma^2}\hat r\) (positive \(= \) repulsive, finite Gaussian width); the log term contributes \(-\lambda/r\,\hat r\) (negative \(= \) attractive, long-range cohesion). Together they create a stable pair equilibrium at \(r_0: \frac{2\gamma}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-r_0^2/\sigma^2} = \lambda/r_0\).
Note: the \(-\gamma\sigma\operatorname{erf}(r)\) potential deepens with separation (erf rises toward 1), which is sometimes loosely called an attractive well in the energy picture, but the associated force is repulsive. A reviewer should interpret \(\Phi_{\text{pair}}\) through forces, not just the potential value.

Pairs interact via a repulsion–attraction potential: Gaussian soft-core repulsion balancing logarithmic long-range cohesion:

Pairwise interaction energy
\[ \Phi_{\text{pair}}(X) = \sum_{i < j} \Bigl[ -\gamma\,\sigma\,\operatorname{erf}\!\Bigl(\frac{r_{ij}}{\sigma}\Bigr) + \lambda\,\ln(r_{ij} + \varepsilon) \Bigr] \]

§ IV

Restoring Forces

3.1 — Radial (Gradient of Spring)

Radial force on agent i
\[ F_i^{\text{rad}} = -\nabla_{x_i}\Phi_{\text{rad}} = -\alpha\,(x_i - \mu) \]

3.2 — Pairwise Force Decomposition

Differentiating the erf-log interaction with respect to \(x_i\):

Pairwise force on agent i from all j ≠ i
\[ F_i^{\text{pair}} = \sum_{j \neq i} \underbrace{\left[ \gamma\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,e^{-(r_{ij}/\sigma)^2} - \frac{\lambda}{r_{ij}} \right]}_{\text{scalar radial coefficient}} \cdot \hat{r}_{ij} \] where \(\hat{r}_{ij} = \dfrac{x_i - x_j}{r_{ij}}\) is the unit separation vector.

Note: The first term is the derivative of \(\operatorname{erf}(r/\sigma)\) with respect to \(r\), giving the Gaussian kernel \(\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-r^2/\sigma^2}\). Since \(F_i=-\nabla_{x_i}\Phi\) and the erf potential deepens with increasing \(r\), the resulting force points outward (repulsive). This kernel should not be called an "attraction kernel".

3.3 — Total Force & Clamp

Total restoring force
\[ F_i = F_i^{\text{rad}} + F_i^{\text{pair}}, \qquad \|F_i\| \leq F_{\max} = 100 \]
Closed-form force matrix — replaces O(N²) agent loop
Define the scalar kernel matrix \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\): \[ K_{ij} = \frac{\gamma\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,e^{-D_{ij}^2/\sigma^2} - \lambda/D_{ij}}{D_{ij}}, \quad i\neq j; \qquad K_{ii} = 0 \] Then the full force matrix is: \[ \boxed{F = -\alpha(X - \mathbf{1}\mu^\top) + \left(K \odot \mathbf{1}\right)X - \operatorname{diag}(K\mathbf{1})\,X} \] More explicitly, letting \(\Delta_{ij} = x_i - x_j\): \[ F_i^{\text{pair}} = \sum_{j\neq i} K_{ij}\,\Delta_{ij} = \Bigl(\sum_{j\neq i}K_{ij}\Bigr)x_i - \sum_{j\neq i}K_{ij}\,x_j = \bigl[(K\mathbf{1})_i\bigr]x_i - [KX]_i \] \[ \boxed{F^{\text{pair}} = \operatorname{diag}(K\mathbf{1})\,X - KX = \bigl(\operatorname{diag}(K\mathbf{1}) - K\bigr)X} \] The matrix \(L = \operatorname{diag}(K\mathbf{1}) - K\) is the weighted graph Laplacian of the force network. Total force in one expression: \[ F = -\alpha(X - \mathbf{1}\mu^\top) - LX \]
Inputs needed: Distance matrix \(D\), state \(X\), equilibrium \(\mu\), parameters \(\alpha,\gamma,\sigma,\lambda\)
§ V

Spectral Radius of the Hessian

Power iteration avoids explicitly forming the \(Nd \times Nd\) Hessian. Each step costs two energy evaluations.

The Hessian of \(\Phi\) at \(X\) is approximated by a finite-difference directional second derivative, then the largest eigenvalue is extracted via randomised power iteration:

Finite-difference Hessian-vector product
\[ H \cdot v \;\approx\; \frac{\Phi(X + h\,v) + \Phi(X - h\,v) - 2\,\Phi(X)}{h^2}\,v, \qquad h = 10^{-4} \]
Power iteration (K = 20 steps)
\[ v^{(k+1)} = \frac{H\,v^{(k)}}{\|H\,v^{(k)}\|}, \qquad \lambda_{\max} = \lim_{k\to K}\|H\,v^{(k)}\| \]
Closed-form Hessian — replaces finite-difference power iteration
The Hessian of \(\Phi\) decomposes analytically into radial and pairwise blocks. For the full system the \(Nd\times Nd\) Hessian is: \[ \nabla^2_X \Phi = \alpha\, I_{Nd} + \frac{\partial^2 \Phi_{\text{pair}}}{\partial X^2} \] The pairwise Hessian block between agents \(i\) and \(j\) (\(i\neq j\)) is a \(d\times d\) matrix: \[ H_{ij}^{\text{pair}} = -\frac{\partial F_i^{\text{pair}}}{\partial x_j} = -K_{ij}\,I_d + \Bigl(\tfrac{\partial K_{ij}}{\partial r_{ij}}\Bigr)\frac{\Delta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^\top}{D_{ij}} \] where \(\frac{\partial K_{ij}}{\partial r} = \frac{-4\gamma r}{\sqrt{\pi}\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/\sigma^2} + \frac{\lambda}{r^2}\) (second derivative of the pairwise potential kernel). Analytical upper bound on \(\lambda_{\max}\) — no iteration: \[ \lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2\Phi) \leq \alpha + \max_i \sum_{j\neq i}\left|K_{ij}\right| + \left|\frac{\partial K_{ij}}{\partial r_{ij}}\right| \quad\text{(Gershgorin row-sum bound)} \] Critical manifold test without eigensolver: \[ \lambda_{\max} > 1 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \alpha + \max_i\sum_{j\neq i}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\gamma}{\sqrt\pi\sigma}e^{-D_{ij}^2/\sigma^2} + \tfrac{\lambda}{D_{ij}^2}\Bigr) > 1 \] This is a single pass over the distance matrix — no simulation loop, no matrix factorisation.
Inputs needed: Distance matrix \(D\), parameters \(\alpha,\gamma,\sigma,\lambda\) — purely from data snapshot \(X_t\)

Critical Manifold \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{man}}\)

The system is supercritical — above the collapse threshold — when:

\[ \lambda_{\max}(H_\Phi(X)) > 1 \]

This is a level-set condition in configuration space. The critical manifold separates the basin of attraction from the unstable regime:

\[ \mathcal{C}_{\text{man}} = \bigl\{X \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d} : \lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2_X\,\Phi) = 1\bigr\} \]

Fraction of trajectory above \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{man}}\):

\[ \hat{p}_{\text{SC}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\lambda_{\max}^{(t)} > 1\right] \]

5.1 — Mahalanobis Ellipsoid (δ_C)

The iso-deviation surfaces are ellipsoids in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) defined by \(\Sigma_0\):

Mahalanobis level set at radius c
\[ \mathcal{E}_c = \bigl\{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : (x-\mu_0)^\top \Sigma_0^{-1}(x-\mu_0) = c^2\bigr\} \] The axes of \(\mathcal{E}_c\) are the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma_0\), with half-lengths \(c\sqrt{\lambda_k^{\Sigma}}\).

5.2 — PCA Decomposition (δ_G)

Let \(V_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\) be the top-\(k\) eigenvectors of \(\Sigma_0\). The deviation decomposes as:

Projection decomposition
\[ \|x_i - \mu_0\|^2 = \underbrace{\|V_k^\top(x_i-\mu_0)\|^2}_{\text{principal subspace}} + \underbrace{\delta_G^{(i)}}_{\text{gap (residual)}} \] \[ \delta_G^{(i)} = \|(I - V_k V_k^\top)(x_i - \mu_0)\|^2 \]

5.3 — KDE Temporal Novelty (δ_T)

KDE density estimate (Gaussian kernel, bandwidth h)
\[ \hat{p}_h(x) = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{s=1}^{H} \frac{1}{(2\pi h^2)^{d/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x - x_{t-s}\|^2}{2h^2}\right) \] \[ \delta_T^{(i)} = \max\!\left(0,\; -\log\hat{p}_h(x_t^{(i)})\right) \] Scott's rule: \(h = H^{-1/(d+4)}\)

MFLS (Mahalanobis Field Line Score)
\[ \text{MFLS}(X) = \left\|\nabla_X E_{\text{BSDT}}(X)\right\|_F = \left\|2\,(X - \mu_0\mathbf{1}^\top)\,\Sigma_0^{-1}\right\|_F \] where \(\nabla_{x_i} E = 2\,\Sigma_0^{-1}(x_i - \mu_0)\) for each agent \(i\).
Closed-form MFLS — single matrix operation
\[ \boxed{\text{MFLS}(X) = 2\,\|\tilde X\,\Sigma_0^{-1}\|_F = 2\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl(\Sigma_0^{-1}\tilde X^\top \tilde X\,\Sigma_0^{-1}\bigr)}} \] Pre-compute \(A = \tilde X\,\Sigma_0^{-1}\) (one \(N\times d\) matrix multiply using the stored inverse), then \(\text{MFLS} = 2\|A\|_F\). No per-timestep loop, no gradient computation at runtime. Optimal damping directly from BSDT energy (correct form):

Note: \(\tfrac{1}{4}\text{MFLS}^2 = \|\tilde X\Sigma_0^{-1}\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_0^{-1}\tilde X^\top\tilde X\Sigma_0^{-1})\) whereas \(E_{\text{BSDT}} = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_0^{-1}\tilde X^\top\tilde X)\). Inputs needed: \(\tilde X = X - \mathbf{1}\mu_0^\top\), pre-factored \(\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\), cost \(\theta\)

§ VIII

Optimal Adaptive Damping \(\gamma^*\)

The optimal intervention intensity balances BSDT blind-spot energy against a cost parameter \(\theta\):

Optimal damping policy
\[ \gamma^*(X_t) = \frac{E_{\text{BSDT}}(X_t)}{E_{\text{BSDT}}(X_t) + \theta}, \qquad \theta = 1 \text{ (intervention cost)} \]

\(\gamma^* \in [0,1)\) is a smooth saturation function of blind-spot energy — it approaches 1 as risk diverges and is 0 when the system is at normal-period baseline.

Geometric & Trigonometric Summary of Collapse

System collapse can be read as a sequence of geometric events in the configuration manifold \(\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\), beginning with the network losing its independence structure and ending with full intervention:

Collapse event sequence — network to intervention

The correct term uses the state-space pullback \(\tilde G_t = \mathbf{J}_t^\top g_t \in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\) (§XVI.2–§XVI.3), giving \(\langle\tilde G_t,\,dW_t\rangle_F\in\mathbb{R}\).

The Itô correction \(\frac{\sigma_n^2}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\nabla^2_X E_{\text{BS}})\) is always positive — noise systematically \emph{inflates} energy on average, pushing the system toward the critical manifold even when the deterministic drift is stabilising.
Noise-adjusted stability condition
\[ \text{Stable in expectation} \iff \langle g_t, F_t\rangle(1-\gamma_t^*) < -\frac{\sigma_n^2}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\nabla^2_X E_{\text{BS}}) \] The deterministic stabilising force must overcome the noise injection. 19.3 — Fokker-Planck Equation

Probability density evolution over the energy landscape
\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(e,t) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial e}\!\Bigl[\mu_e(e)\,p\Bigr] + \frac{\sigma_n^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial e^2} \] \[ \mu_e(e) = \langle g, F\rangle(1-\gamma^*(e)) + \frac{\sigma_n^2}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\nabla^2 E_{\text{BS}}) \] The stationary distribution (if it exists) satisfies: \[ p^*(e) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{2}{\sigma_n^2}\int^e \mu_e(e')\,de'\right) \] This is the probability density of the energy level — the distribution of where the system spends its time. A heavy tail toward high \(e\) signals elevated systemic risk even in the "normal" regime.

19.4 — Stochastic Critical Manifold

Probability of crossing C_man within time τ
\[ P(\tau_{\text{cross}} < \tau) \approx 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\text{Kramers}}}\right) \] \[ \tau_{\text{Kramers}} = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{|\mu_e'(e_t)|\cdot|\mu_e'(e^*)|}}\cdot\exp\!\left(\frac{2(e^* - e_t)}{\sigma_n^2}\right) \] Approximation note. The standard Kramers rate (overdamped 1D) is \(\tau = (2\pi/\sqrt{|V''(x_{\min})||V''(x_{\max})|})\ exp(2\Delta V/\sigma_n^2)\) where primes denote second derivatives at the potential minimum and maximum. The formula above uses \(|\mu_e'|\) (first derivatives of the drift function) as a proxy for those curvatures — valid when \(\mu_e(e)\) is well-approximated by a linear function near each point, which holds near a saddle but not in general. Treat this as a qualitative order-of-magnitude estimate, not a precise first-passage formula. Noise reduces this time by shrinking the effective barrier. Monitoring \((e^*-e_t)/\sigma_n^2\) gives a probabilistic collapse horizon.
§ XX

The Inverse Problem — Parameter Identification from Collapse

The forward problem is: given parameters, predict collapse. The inverse problem is: given an observed collapse trajectory, what parameters caused it? This is the identification problem — it tells you not just that collapse happened, but \emph{which} structural features of the system were responsible.

20.1 — The Observable

Observed trajectory residual
\[ R_t(\theta_{\text{param}}) = \dot X_t^{\text{obs}} - F_t(\theta_{\text{param}}) + \gamma_t^*\langle F_t,u_t\rangle u_t \] where \(\theta_{\text{param}} = (\alpha, \gamma, \sigma, \lambda, A, \beta, w)\) is the full parameter vector. At the true parameters, \(R_t = \sigma_n\,\dot W_t\) (pure noise).

20.2 — Least-Squares Identification

Parameter identification objective
\[ \hat\theta = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{t=1}^T \|R_t(\theta)\|_F^2 = \arg\min_\theta \sum_t \|\dot X_t - F_t(\theta) + \gamma_t^*(\theta)\langle F_t(\theta), u_t(\theta)\rangle u_t(\theta)\|_F^2 \] The gradient with respect to each parameter group: \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \alpha} = -2\sum_t \langle R_t,\; \tilde X_{t,\mu}\rangle \quad\text{(radial spring identification)} \] \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial A} = -4\sum_t \langle R_t,\; S_t S_t^\top\rangle \quad\text{(Quadsurf coupling identification)} \]

20.3 — Collapse Attribution

Attribution score — which parameter contributed most to collapse
\[ \mathcal{A}_k = \left\|\frac{\partial \dot X_t}{\partial \theta_k}\bigg|_{\hat\theta}\right\|_F \cdot |\hat\theta_k| \] Normalised attribution: \[ \bar{\mathcal{A}}_k = \frac{\mathcal{A}_k}{\sum_j \mathcal{A}_j} \in [0,1], \qquad \sum_k \bar{\mathcal{A}}_k = 1 \] This is a sensitivity-weighted parameter importance score — the first formal answer to "what broke first?" derived directly from the master operator.
§ XXI

Agent Sensitivity — Who Pushes the System Across \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{man}}\)

Not all agents are equally dangerous. Some agents, if perturbed, cause disproportionate increase in \(\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2\Phi)\). The sensitivity of the spectral threshold to each agent's state identifies the systemically important institutions — those whose individual deviation most rapidly destabilises the whole.

21.1 — Spectral Sensitivity

Gradient of λ_max with respect to agent i's state
By the matrix perturbation identity (Hadamard first variation): \[ \frac{\partial \lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2\Phi)}{\partial x_i} = v_1^\top \frac{\partial \nabla^2\Phi}{\partial x_i}\, v_1 \] where \(v_1\) is the principal eigenvector of \(\nabla^2\Phi\). The \(d\)-vector above is the \emph{systemic importance vector} of agent \(i\) — the direction in which agent \(i\) must move to most rapidly push \(\lambda_{\max}\) above 1.
Scalar systemic importance score per agent
\[ \mathcal{S}_i = \left\|\frac{\partial \lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2\Phi)}{\partial x_i}\right\| = \left\|v_1^\top \frac{\partial \nabla^2\Phi}{\partial x_i}\, v_1\right\| \] \[ \text{Normalised: } \bar{\mathcal{S}}_i = \frac{\mathcal{S}_i}{\sum_j \mathcal{S}_j} \] The agent with \(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_i = \max_j \bar{\mathcal{S}}_j\) is the \emph{critical institution} — a Basel III macro-prudential target derived analytically from the system geometry.

21.2 — Network Amplification of Systemic Importance

Amplification through the Laplacian network
\[ \frac{\partial F_t}{\partial x_i} = -\alpha\,I_d - \frac{\partial L_t}{\partial x_i}\,X_t - L_t\,e_i e_i^\top \] where \(e_i\) is the \(i\)-th standard basis vector in \(\mathbb{R}^N\). The term \(\partial L_t/\partial x_i\) captures how agent \(i\)'s movement changes the force network topology — it is the feedback loop between individual state and collective coupling.
§ XXII

Recovery Dynamics & Hysteresis

Collapse and recovery are not time-reversals of each other. The erf-log potential has multiple local minima — once the system falls into a crisis basin, the return path is geometrically different from the collapse path. This asymmetry is hysteresis, and it is encoded exactly in the energy landscape.

22.1 — Multiple Equilibria

Equilibrium condition — zeros of the force field
\[ F(X^*) = 0 \iff -\alpha(X^* - \mathbf{1}\mu^\top) = L_{X^*}\,X^* \] This is a nonlinear fixed-point equation. The number of solutions depends on the pairwise coupling strength \(\gamma\) relative to the radial spring \(\alpha\). There is always a trivial solution at \(X^* = \mu\mathbf{1}^\top\); additional crisis equilibria exist when: \[ \lambda_{\max}(L_{X^*}) > \alpha \]
Critical coupling for multiple equilibria
\[ \gamma_{\text{crit}} = \frac{\alpha\,\sigma\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\max_i\sum_{j\neq i} e^{-D_{ij}^{*2}/\sigma^2}} \] For \(\gamma > \gamma_{\text{crit}}\), crisis equilibria exist. The system can be trapped in a collapsed state even after the original shock is removed.

22.2 — Hysteresis Loop

Energy barrier for collapse vs. recovery
\[ \Delta\Phi_{\text{collapse}} = \Phi(X_{\text{saddle}}) - \Phi(X_{\text{normal}}) \] \[ \Delta\Phi_{\text{recovery}} = \Phi(X_{\text{saddle}}) - \Phi(X_{\text{crisis}}) \] Since \(\Phi(X_{\text{normal}}) < \Phi(X_{\text{crisis}})\) for deep crises, the recovery barrier is \emph{smaller} — the system recovers more easily than it collapsed. But the crisis state is a different point in phase space, so the recovery path follows a different trajectory.
Recovery control — the reverse master operator
\[ \dot X_t^{\text{recovery}} = F_t + \gamma_t^{\text{rec}}\,\langle F_t, u_t^{\text{rec}}\rangle\,u_t^{\text{rec}} \] where \(u_t^{\text{rec}} = -u_t\) (point \emph{toward} the normal basin rather than away from the crisis direction). The recovery operator is the sign-reversed control law — it actively accelerates motion toward \(\mu_0\) rather than merely damping motion away from it.
§ XXIII

Information-Theoretic Interpretation — BSDT as Divergence

The BSDT energy \(E_{\text{BS}}\) is not merely an ad-hoc distance measure. Under a Gaussian normal-period model, it is exactly a KL divergence — giving the blind-spot energy a rigorous information-theoretic foundation, a statistical interpretation, and a natural calibration procedure with confidence intervals.

23.1 — The Normal-Period Model

Gaussian baseline — normal period distribution
\[ p_0(x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_0,\, \Sigma_0) \propto \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu_0)^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}(x-\mu_0)\right) \]

23.2 — BSDT Energy as KL Divergence

Mahalanobis energy = log-likelihood ratio = KL divergence component
\[ \delta_C^{(i)} = (x_i - \mu_0)^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}(x_i-\mu_0) = -2\log\frac{p_0(x_i)}{p_0(\mu_0)} = \chi^2_d \] Under the null (normal period), \(\delta_C^{(i)} \sim \chi^2_d\) — a chi-squared distribution with \(d\) degrees of freedom. The total energy: \[ e_t = \sum_i \delta_C^{(i)} \sim \chi^2_{Nd} \quad\text{under } H_0 \text{ IF agents are independent} \]

Reviewer caution. The \(\chi^2_{Nd}\) distribution requires agents \(x_t^{(i)}\) to be drawn i.i.d.\(\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_0,\Sigma_0)\). In the BSDT/GravityEngine model, agents are correlated (that is the entire point of §II); their joint distribution under \(H_0\) is \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_0\otimes\mathbf{1}, \hat\Sigma_{\text{joint}})\) where \(\hat\Sigma_{\text{joint}}\) is the full \(Nd\times Nd\) joint covariance, not a block-diagonal of \(N\) copies of \(\Sigma_0\). Therefore \(e_t\) under \(H_0\) is a general quadratic form in correlated normals — distributed as \(\sum_k \lambda_k^{(joint)}\chi^2_1(k)\), not \(\chi^2_{Nd}\). The \(\chi^2_{Nd}\) threshold \(e^*\) is valid only in the limiting regime \(T\gg N\) where empirical agent correlations are negligible, or when \(N=1\). For production use with \(N\geq 4\), calibrate \(e^*\) from the empirical distribution of \(e_t\) in the normal period rather than from the \(\chi^2\) quantile.

KL divergence from empirical to normal-period distribution
\[ D_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(\hat p_t \,\|\, p_0\bigr) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (x_t^{(i)}-\mu_0)^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}(x_t^{(i)}-\mu_0) + \text{const} = \frac{e_t}{N} + \text{const} \] \(E_{\text{BS}}\) is proportional to the KL divergence between the current empirical distribution and the normal-period distribution. A larger \(e_t\) means more information-theoretic "surprise" — the system has moved into a region that was improbable during normal times.

23.3 — Statistical Threshold from Chi-Squared

Calibrated collapse threshold with confidence level
\[ e^*(\alpha_{\text{conf}}) = \chi^2_{Nd,\,1-\alpha_{\text{conf}}} \] The critical energy level is the \((1-\alpha_{\text{conf}})\) quantile of the chi-squared distribution. For \(\alpha_{\text{conf}} = 0.01\) (1\% false-positive rate with \(N=10\), \(d=4\)): \[ e^*(0.01) = \chi^2_{40,\,0.99} \approx 63.7 \] This replaces the heuristic threshold \(\lambda_{\max} > 1\) with a statistically principled one: the critical manifold is the \((1-\alpha)\) confidence ellipsoid of the normal-period distribution.

23.4 — MFLS as Fisher Information

MFLS squared = trace of Fisher information matrix
\[ \text{MFLS}^2 = \|\nabla_X E_{\text{BS}}\|_F^2 = 4\|\tilde X_t\Sigma_0^{-1}\|_F^2 = 4\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl(\Sigma_0^{-1}\tilde X_t^\top \tilde X_t\,\Sigma_0^{-1}\bigr) \] This is \(4\) times the trace of the sample Fisher information matrix at the current state relative to the Gaussian normal-period model. High MFLS means the current state is in a region of high curvature of the log-likelihood — the system is far from where the normal-period model has mass, and small perturbations cause large changes in log-probability.

23.5 — Unified Energy as Composite Divergence

E_BS as sum of divergence measures
\[ E_{\text{BS}}(S) = \underbrace{S^\top A S}_{\text{Mahalanobis (KL, Gaussian)}} + \underbrace{\sum_k e^{\beta_k S_k} - 1 - \beta_k S_k}_{\text{Bregman divergence (exponential family)}} + \underbrace{w^\top S}_{\text{linear tilt (exponential family reweighting)}} \] The Expogate term \(\sum_k e^{\beta_k S_k}\) minus its Taylor expansion \(1 + \beta_k S_k\) is exactly the Bregman divergence generated by the exponential function — the excess over the linear approximation. The Signed LR term is an exponential family tilting operator that shifts the reference distribution toward higher-risk states. Together, \(E_{\text{BS}}\) is a composite divergence measuring: how far \(X_t\) is from the normal-period mean (Mahalanobis), how nonlinear that deviation is (Bregman), and in which direction it is biased (tilt).

\[ \dot V_k^{\text{drift}} = [g_t]_k\,\langle\partial_X\delta_k, F_t\rangle_F \] \[ \dot V_k^{\text{control}} = -[g_t]_k\,\frac{\gamma_t^*\langle F_t, g_t\rangle}{\|g_t\|^2}\,\langle\partial_X\delta_k, g_t\rangle_F \] \[ \dot V_k = \dot V_k^{\text{drift}} + \dot V_k^{\text{control}} \]

25.3 — Channel Stability Attribution

Fraction of total instability rate driven by channel k
\[ a_k = \frac{\max(0, \dot V_k)}{\sum_j \max(0, \dot V_j)}, \qquad \sum_k a_k = 1 \] \(a_k\) identifies the dominant channel driving current instability — not the channel with the highest deviation score, but the channel whose spatial gradient is most aligned with the current force field. These are different, and the difference matters: a channel can have a high score (\([g_t]_k\) large) but contribute little to instability (\(\dot V_k \approx 0\)) if its spatial gradient is perpendicular to the force.
Control efficiency per channel
\[ \eta_k = \frac{-\dot V_k^{\text{control}}}{\dot V_k^{\text{drift}}} = \frac{\gamma_t^*\langle F_t, g_t\rangle\,\langle\partial_X\delta_k, g_t\rangle_F}{\|g_t\|^2\,\langle\partial_X\delta_k, F_t\rangle_F} \] \(\eta_k \in (0,1)\): control is partially offsetting channel \(k\)'s drift. \(\eta_k > 1\): control overcorrects channel \(k\) — the intervention is stronger than the drift for this channel. \(\eta_k < 0\): the control is making channel \(k\) worse — the projection direction is misaligned with channel \(k\)'s spatial gradient. Negative \(\eta_k\) is the per-channel manifestation of the uncontrollable regime: the control law, while stabilising the dominant channel, is actively destabilising channel \(k\).

25.4 — The Control Coupling Sum \(R_t\)

R_t — how well the control direction reaches each channel
\[ R_t = \sum_k [g_t]_k\,\langle\partial_X\delta_k, g_t\rangle_F \] \(R_t > 0\): the control direction \(g_t\) is positively coupled to the physical gradient of each channel — control stabilises. \(R_t \leq 0\): the control direction is orthogonal to or opposed by the channel spatial gradients — the uncontrollable regime. \(R_t\) is the single scalar that determines whether the system is in the controllable or uncontrollable regime.

The \(\psi_t\) Angle — The Sixth Diagnostic Signal

Five diagnostic signals were established across §V–XVII: MFLS, \(\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2\Phi)\), \(\rho(W)\), \(\gamma^*\), \(\cos\theta_t\). 26.1 — Definition

ψ_t — the misalignment angle
\[ \cos\psi_t = \frac{\langle\tilde{G}_t,\,g_t\rangle_F}{\|\tilde{G}_t\|_F\,\|g_t\|} \in [-1, 1] \] \[ \psi_t = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\sum_k [g_t]_k\,\langle\partial_X\delta_k, g_t\rangle_F}{\|\tilde{G}_t\|_F\,\|g_t\|}\right) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{R_t}{\|\tilde{G}_t\|_F\,\|g_t\|}\right) \]

\[ \boxed{\cos\psi_t \;=\; \rho^{\text{n}}_t \;=\; \frac{\|\tilde{G}_t\|_F}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\text{Gram}_t)}\,\|g_t\|} \;\in\; [0,1]} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \psi_t = \arccos\rho^{\text{n}}_t \]

Yet their sum \(F_t = -\gamma\,T(g_t) + \dots\) is positively aligned with \(\Delta X_t\). This is the precise content of the master operator: the alpha is the composition, not any single channel. Per-channel decomposition, magnitude-free per-channel gates, and channel-by-channel attribution all destroy the geometric cancellation that produces forecast skill. This explains the §28 finding that magnitude-free gates (\(\text{eff\_rank}_{\text{corr}}\), sign-only \(D\)) underperform raw §XXIV gates by \(0.05\!-\!0.13\) Sharpe — they are honest measurements of the wrong object.

Theoretical statement. Let \(\Pi_k = \langle\,\cdot\,,J_k\rangle_F J_k/\|J_k\|_F^2\) be the projection onto channel \(k\). Then

\[ \sum_k \bigl\langle [g_t]_k J_k,\,\Delta X_t\bigr\rangle_F \;=\; \bigl\langle T(g_t),\,\Delta X_t\bigr\rangle_F \;\neq\; \bigl\langle F_t,\,\Delta X_t\bigr\rangle_F \quad\text{in general,} \]

and the cross-channel covariance \(\sum_{k\neq j}[g_t]_k[g_t]_j\langle J_k,J_j\rangle_F\) is the source of the sign flip. Magnitude-weighted single-channel readouts approximate the diagonal of this form; the off-diagonal structure (carried only by the full operator \(T\) acting jointly with the friction \(-\gamma\)) is where the predictive content lives.

Trading consequence. The naïve readout \(\mathrm{sgn}\langle F_t,u_t^{\text{state}}\rangle\) of §24.6 has IC \(\approx 0\) (cf. §24.6 amendment); the gates of §26 have positive Sharpe because they admit/reject bars on which the geometry is admissible, but lose information by collapsing the field to a binary decision. The next leverage point in trading layers is therefore not a sharper scalar gate but a geometric extractor — a map \(F_t\mapsto\hat r_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}\) (or \(\hat{\Delta X}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\)) that preserves the multi-channel composition rather than collapsing to one rank-1 direction.

26.6 — Catalogue of geometric extractors

⚠ Research catalogue — admissible \(F_t\mapsto\hat{\Delta X}_{t+1}\) maps

All extractors below take the engine field \(F_t\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\) and the panel history \(\{X_s\}_{s\le t}\) as input and emit either a direction in state space (then traded by \(\langle\hat{\Delta X}, e_{\text{ETH}}\rangle\)) or directly a scalar return forecast. We list eight classes ordered by structural information used.

A. Directional projection extractors

  1. E1 — Realised-\(\Delta X\) PCA basis. Let \(\{v_1,\dots,v_r\}\) be the top-\(r\) right-singular directions of the rolling matrix \([\Delta X_{t-N+1}\,|\,\dots\,|\,\Delta X_t]\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd\times N}\). Forecast: \[ \hat{\Delta X}_{t+1} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{r}\bigl\langle F_t,\,v_i\bigr\rangle_F\,v_i. \] Replaces the rank-1 \(\tilde G_t\) direction with a data-driven \(r\)-frame in the subspace where ΔX actually lives. Hyper-params: window \(N\), rank \(r\) (start \(N=128, r=3\) per Sec 12B \(\text{eff\_rank}_{\text{corr}}\!\approx\!3\)).
  2. E2 — Channel-Jacobian frame (\(\{J_C,J_G,J_A,J_T\}\)). Build the orthonormal frame \(\{q_k\}\) by Gram-Schmidt on \(\{J_k/\|J_k\|_F\}\) (correlation-Gram normalisation, §24.4 amendment). Forecast: \[ \hat{\Delta X}_{t+1} \;=\; \sum_k\alpha_k(t)\,q_k(t),\qquad \alpha_k(t)=\langle F_t,q_k\rangle_F\cdot\hat\beta_k, \] with \(\hat\beta_k\) a per-channel calibration coefficient fitted offline (sign-corrects the negative per-channel cosines of §26.5).
  3. E3 — Whitened state inner product. Use the BSDT inverse covariance \(\Sigma_0^{-1}\): \[ \hat r_{t+1} \;=\; \langle F_t,\,\Sigma_0^{-1}\,\Delta X_{t-1}\rangle_F. \] Mahalanobis-aligned readout — projects \(F_t\) along the most-recent \(\Delta X\) after whitening by the BSDT covariance, so the C-channel scale is removed natively.

B. Bilinear / quadratic extractors

  1. E4 — Cross-channel covariance form. Predict from the off-diagonal Gram entries that §26.5 identified as the source of the sign-flip: \[ \hat r_{t+1} \;=\; \sum_{k\neq j}[g_t]_k[g_t]_j\,W_{kj}\,\langle J_k,J_j\rangle_F,\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}. \] The diagonal of \(W\) is fixed to zero; off-diagonal weights fitted in-sample. Six free parameters.
  2. E5 — Bilinear neural form \(F_t \,W\, J\). Generalise E4 to a learned \(d\!\times\!d\) bilinear form: \[ \hat{\Delta X}_{t+1} \;=\; F_t\,W\,(\partial_X\delta_C(X_t))^{\!\top},\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}. \] Strict generalisation of \(\langle F_t,u^{\text{state}}\rangle\) (recover by \(W=I\)). Fit by ridge regression on \(\{F_t,\Delta X_t\}\).
  3. E6 — Energy-flux extractor. Use the Lyapunov derivative content directly: \[ \hat r_{t+1} \;=\; -\,\dot V_t \;=\; -\bigl\langle F_t,\,\nabla_X V(X_t)\bigr\rangle_F,\qquad V = \tfrac{1}{2}\|\tilde X\|_{\Sigma_0^{-1}}^2. \] Forecast = instantaneous rate of energy descent. Sign of \(-\dot V\) predicts whether the state will move toward or away from the BSDT mean.

C. Memory / accumulator extractors

  1. E7 — EMA-aligned force \(\bar F_t\). Let \(\bar F_t = (1-\lambda)\bar F_{t-1} + \lambda F_t\). Forecast: \[ \hat{\Delta X}_{t+1} \;=\; \bar F_t\quad\text{or}\quad \hat r_{t+1} = \langle\bar F_t,\,\Delta X_{t-1}\rangle_F. \] Trades the high-frequency cancellation against persistence; \(\lambda\) chosen so the AR(1) anti-persistence (\(-0.41\) in §26.5) is partly absorbed.
  2. E8 — Path-integrated force. Forecast from the cumulative force over a window \(W\): \[ \hat{\Delta X}_{t+1} \;=\; \frac{1}{W}\sum_{s=t-W+1}^{t} F_s, \] a Wiener-style time-averaged drift. Cancels orthogonal high-frequency components and exposes the slow-manifold component.

D. Adaptive / learned extractors

  1. E9 — State-space Kalman filter. Treat \(F_t\) as a noisy observation of the latent drift \(\mu_t\) in \(\Delta X_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t\); Kalman-update \(\mu_t\) bar-by-bar with process noise \(Q\) and observation noise \(R\) calibrated from in-sample residuals. Forecast \(\hat{\Delta X}_{t+1} = \hat\mu_t\).
  2. E10 — Tangent-bundle regression on \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{man}}\). Fit a low-degree polynomial \(\hat{\Delta X}_{t+1} = \Phi(X_t,F_t)\theta\) where \(\Phi\) is a feature map respecting the BSDT manifold tangent (e.g. \([F_t,\,X_t\!\odot\!F_t,\,\|F_t\|_F\,F_t]\)) and \(\theta\) is fit by ridge on rolling windows. Captures position-dependent gain that scalar gates cannot.

29.1 — Per-Channel Collapse Attribution

Given the k=1 PCA projection of the state matrix, the k-th channel signal is \(\tilde S_k\). The mean-normalised attribution for channel \(k\) at time \(t\) is:

\[ a_k(t) = \frac{\tilde S_k(t)^2}{\|\tilde S(t)\|^2}, \qquad \tilde S_k = \frac{S_k}{\mu_k^{(\text{normal})}}, \qquad \sum_k a_k = 1 \]

The mean-normalisation \(\mu_k^{(\text{normal})}\) is estimated once on the calibration window and frozen. It converts raw channel magnitudes into the fraction of total anomaly energy carried by each channel. \(a_G \approx 0.45\) means 45% of the current deviation from normal lives in the gap (PCA out-of-plane) channel.

The following results hold for any domain where the BSDT framework is instantiated and a measurable volatility/dispersion regime exists:

Generalisation results

G1. Parameter plateau ≠ signal exhaustion. When all scalar parameter axes are exhausted (Δ < 0.002 Sharpe per perturbation), the correct response is not more parameter search but promotion of parameters to state-dependent policies.

G5. Walk-forward OOS + lag corruption are the two non-negotiable tests. Shuffle confirms significance. Formula robustness confirms form stability.
