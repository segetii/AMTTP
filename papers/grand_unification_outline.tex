\begin{abstract}

\textbf{[OUTLINE — to be expanded into full paper]}

We demonstrate that Blind-Spot Decomposition Theory (BSDT) --- originally
developed to diagnose and correct fraud detection failures --- constructs
a \emph{reusable geometry of system collapse} applicable, under structurally
verifiable conditions, across seven fundamentally
different domains: (1)~anomaly detection in machine learning,
(2)~systemic risk in financial and energy networks, (3)~regularity of
the 3D incompressible Navier--Stokes equations, (4)~gradient stability
in deep neural network training, (5)~combinatorial satisfiability at
the random 3-SAT phase transition, (6)~supply chain disruption
prediction and resilience control, and (7)~quantum phase transition
detection in many-body systems.

The claim is \emph{geometric}, not that every domain shares the same
literal physical energy law.  BSDT maps each system into a common
multi-view tensor representation and induces a \emph{surrogate
instability landscape} in which collapse becomes observable,
attributable, and controllable.  In \emph{physical} domains (fluid
mechanics, quantum systems), this geometry aligns with an established
energy or Hamiltonian structure.  In \emph{non-physical} domains
(banking, fraud, optimisation, supply chains), it functions as a
mathematically constructed instability landscape for the modelled
system.

The unifying mechanism is twofold.  First, a \emph{four-channel energy
functional} $\Ebs = \sum_k w_k \psi_k(\delta_k)$ decomposes structural
instability into geometrically interpretable components (Camouflage,
Feature Gap, Activity Anomaly, Temporal Novelty).  Second, an
\emph{adaptive friction law} $\gamma^*(X) = \alpha / \lambda_{\max}
(D^2\Phi)$ stabilises any coupled dynamical system above a critical
manifold $\Cman$ where fixed parameters provably fail.  Under five
explicitly stated, structurally verifiable assumptions, $\Ebs$ shares
the topological structure of the induced system potential $\Phi$
(identical Morse index, homotopy-equivalent sublevel sets, two-sided
gradient and energy bounds); where these assumptions weaken globally,
$\Ebs$ degrades gracefully to a surrogate energy while stability is
maintained by damping dominance.

We prove that the double-well binarisation potential $\mu(1-s^2)^2$
connecting continuous relaxation to discrete decisions is the common
mathematical object across all seven domains, and provide empirical
validation in each: MFLS AUC 0.639--0.982 (fraud), 6-quarter GFC
early warning (finance), bounded enstrophy at $\mathrm{Re} \approx
62{,}832$ (Navier--Stokes), 0/8 BSDT variants blow up where SGD/RMSProp diverge at epoch~1, with
training loss $53{,}600\times$ lower than constant clipping
(neural training), 94--100\% solve rate at $\alpha=4.0$ random 3-SAT
(combinatorial), zero cascade depth across 5 historical supply chain
disruptions where constant buffers fail (COVID semiconductor, Suez,
Fukushima, Thai floods, Texas ERCOT), and MFLS AUC 0.973 for quantum
phase transition detection in the 1D transverse-field Ising model
($N=12$, zero domain knowledge, ranking \#5 of 22 standard methods).

\end{abstract}

% ── 2.1b Applicable System Class ──────────────────────────
\subsection{Applicable System Class}\label{sec:system_class}

Before stating the assumptions and theorems, we define the class of
systems to which BSDT applies.  This addresses the question: \emph{for
which systems should one expect A1--A5 to hold, a priori?}

\begin{definition}[BSDT-admissible system]\label{def:admissible}
A \textbf{BSDT-admissible system} is a triple
$(\mathcal{X}, \Phi, \{\delta_k\}_{k=1}^K)$ satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Coupled gradient flow.}  The state $X \in \mathcal{X}
    \subseteq \R^{Nd}$ evolves under $\dot{X} = -\grad\Phi(X) + F_{\mathrm{pair}}(X)$,
    where $\Phi$ is a $C^2$ coercive potential with compact sublevel sets.
  \item \textbf{Metastable equilibria.}  $\Phi$ has at least one strict
    local minimum $\Xst$ ($D^2\Phi(\Xst) \succ 0$) and at least one
    saddle point ($\ind_\Phi \ge 1$), separated by an energy barrier.
  \item \textbf{Pairwise coupling.}  The interaction term
    $F_{\mathrm{pair}} = -\grad\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}$ derives from
    a pairwise potential whose spectral radius creates the critical
    manifold $\Cman$.
  \item \textbf{Lipschitz deviation channels.}  The channels
    $\delta_k: \mathcal{X} \to \R^+$ are $C^1$ and $K_k$-Lipschitz
    on sublevel sets, measuring complementary geometric deviations
    from the reference distribution.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[A priori applicability]
\label{rem:apriori}
Definition~\ref{def:admissible} is \emph{predictive}, not merely
diagnostic.  One can determine whether a system is BSDT-admissible
\emph{before} running the framework, by checking:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Is the dynamics a gradient flow (or approximately so)?  This
    holds for Hamiltonian systems with dissipation, energy-minimising
    agents, and discretised PDEs with energy structure.
  \item Does the system have multiple equilibria (metastability)?
    Present in phase-separating systems, multi-modal landscapes,
    and bistable networks.
  \item Is there pairwise coupling whose strength varies?
    Present whenever agents, particles, or nodes interact and the
    coupling intensity is state-dependent.
\end{itemize}
All seven domains in this paper are manifestly BSDT-admissible:
financial institutions coupled by exposures, fluid parcels coupled
by advection, neurons coupled by gradient covariance, SAT variables
coupled by shared clauses, supply chain nodes coupled by dependency
links, and quantum spins coupled by exchange interaction.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Genericity and verifiability of the assumptions]\label{prop:generic}
For a BSDT-admissible system (Definition~\ref{def:admissible}),
the five assumptions decompose into two categories:

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part~I: Generic assumptions (A1--A3, A5).}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item[\textup{(i)}] Assumptions~A\ref{ass:reg}--A\ref{ass:equil}
    (regularity, strict minimum) hold \emph{by definition} of
    admissibility: condition~(i) of Definition~\ref{def:admissible}
    gives $\Phi \in C^2$ coercive, and condition~(ii) gives a strict
    local minimum with $D^2\Phi(X^*) \succ 0$.
  \item[\textup{(ii)}] Assumption~A\ref{ass:bsdt} (BSDT regularity)
    holds \emph{by construction}: the standard channels
    (Mahalanobis, projection residual, local velocity, kNN density)
    are Lipschitz on compact sets (Mahalanobis via smoothness of
    the covariance; projection via $\|P_k\|_{\mathrm{op}} \le 1$;
    velocity via Lipschitz dynamics; kNN density via bounded
    kernel width on compact sets), and the link functions $\psi_k$
    are convex by choice.
  \item[\textup{(iii)}] Assumption~A\ref{ass:morse} (non-degeneracy)
    is \emph{generic in $C^2$}: by the Kupka--Smale theorem,
    the set of $C^2$ functions whose critical points all have
    non-degenerate Hessians is residual (countable intersection of
    open dense sets) in the Whitney $C^2$ topology.  Equivalently,
    degenerate critical points require $\det D^2\Phi(X_c) = 0$,
    a codimension-1 condition; simultaneous degeneracy at all
    critical points has infinite codimension.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Part~II: Verifiable assumption (A4).}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item[\textup{(iv)}] Assumption~A\ref{ass:sep} (separation ---
    non-degeneracy of channel responses plus monotonicity) is
    \emph{not generic in the abstract}: it depends on the
    reference distribution $\mathcal{N}$ and the channel
    definitions.  However, it is \textbf{verifiable a priori}
    via Proposition~\ref{prop:separation}, which gives
    closed-form lower bounds
    $\nu_C \ge f(\kappa(\Sigma_0)),\;
     \nu_G \ge f(\alpha),\;
     \nu_A \ge f(h),\;
     \nu_T \ge f(L_c)$
    as functions of computable properties of the reference data.
    These bounds can be evaluated \emph{before running the framework}.

    Moreover, the failure mode is identifiable: if any
    $\nu_k < \varepsilon$ for small $\varepsilon > 0$, this
    indicates near-degeneracy of channel~$k$ (e.g., an
    isotropic covariance collapsing the Mahalanobis channel),
    which can be diagnosed and corrected by feature engineering
    or channel augmentation.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Summary.}
Assumptions~A1--A3 and A5 hold generically for BSDT-admissible
systems; failure requires codimension-$\ge 1$ degeneracy.
Assumption~A4 is not generic but is \emph{checkable a priori}
from the reference data alone, with explicit sufficient conditions
(Proposition~\ref{prop:separation}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Parts~(i)--(ii) are immediate from the definitions.
Part~(iii): the Kupka--Smale theorem \cite{kupka1963,smale1963}
states that for $C^r$ dynamical systems ($r \ge 2$), the set of
potentials with all critical points non-degenerate is residual
in the Whitney topology.  For our setting, $\Phi \in C^2$
coercive on $\R^{Nd}$; non-degeneracy means
$\det D^2\Phi(X_c) \ne 0$ at each critical point, which is
an open condition.  The closed set of degenerate potentials
is a finite union of hypersurfaces
$\{\Phi : \det D^2\Phi(X_{c,j}) = 0\}$, each of codimension~1.
Their intersection---simultaneous degeneracy at all critical
points---has codimension equal to the number of critical points.
Thus non-degeneracy is generic.

Part~(iv): Proposition~\ref{prop:separation} derives the
separation bounds from the spectral properties of $\Sigma_0$,
the projection deficiency $\alpha$, bandwidth $h$, and
Lipschitz constant $L_c$.  These are computable from the
reference distribution $\mathcal{N}$ without access to the
dynamics.  The claim is verifiability, not genericity.
\end{proof}

% ── 2.2 The Energy Functional ──────────────────────────────
\subsection{Blind-Spot Energy Functional}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ebs}
  \Ebs(X) = \sum_{k=1}^{4} w_k \, \psi_k(\delta_k(X))
  + \Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}(X),
\end{equation}
where $\psi_k:\R^+\to\R^+$ are convex, non-decreasing, 1-Lipschitz
link functions with $\psi_k(0)=0$, the pairwise potential
$\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}$ coincides with the interaction term in the
system dynamics~\eqref{eq:gradflow}, and $w_k$ are Fisher
variance-ratio (VR) weights determined entirely from unlabelled data.

\paragraph{Fisher Variance-Ratio Weighting.}
Let $m_k = |\delta_k(X)|$ be the channel-$k$ magnitude vector.  Split
observations at the 80th and 50th percentiles of total magnitude
$M = \sum_k m_k$ into ``high'' and ``low'' groups and compute
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fisher_vr}
  \mathrm{FR}_k
  = \frac{(\mu_k^{\mathrm{high}} - \mu_k^{\mathrm{low}})^2}
         {\mathrm{Var}_k^{\mathrm{high}}
          + \mathrm{Var}_k^{\mathrm{low}}},
  \qquad
  w_k = \frac{\mathrm{FR}_k}{\sum_{j}\mathrm{FR}_j}.
\end{equation}
This closed-form weighting adaptively assigns importance to
whichever channels best separate high-deviation from low-deviation
regimes, with zero label leakage by construction.

% ── 2.2b Assumptions ──────────────────────────────────────
\paragraph{Assumptions.}
The following five assumptions are inherited from the companion
SIAM paper \cite{odeyemi2026siam}; we verify each in all seven
domains.

\begin{assumption}[Regularity]\label{ass:reg}
$\Phi\in C^2(\R^{N\times d})$ is coercive:
$\Phi(X)\to+\infty$ as $\norm{X}\to\infty$.  Every sublevel set
$\mathcal{L}_c := \{X:\Phi(X)\le c\}$ is compact, with Lipschitz
constant $L_c$ for $\grad\Phi$ on $\mathcal{L}_c$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Strict local minimum]\label{ass:equil}
The equilibrium $\Xst$ satisfies $\grad\Phi(\Xst)=0$ and
$H^*:=D^2\Phi(\Xst)\succ 0$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[BSDT regularity]\label{ass:bsdt}
Each $\delta_k: \R^{N\times d}\to\R^+$ is $C^1$ and
$K_k$-Lipschitz on $\mathcal{L}_c$.  Each $\psi_k:\R^+\to\R^+$
is convex, non-decreasing, 1-Lipschitz, with $\psi_k(0)=0$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Separation]\label{ass:sep}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item[\textup{(S1)}] \textbf{Non-degeneracy.}
    There exists $\nu>0$ such that for every
    $X\in\mathcal{L}_c$ with $\grad\Phi(X)\neq 0$, at least one
    $k\in\{1,\ldots,4\}$ satisfies
    $\norm{\grad_X\delta_k(X)}\ge\nu\norm{\grad\Phi(X)}$.
  \item[\textup{(S2)}] \textbf{Monotonicity.}
    $\inner{\grad_X\delta_k(X)}{\grad\Phi(X)}\ge 0$ for all
    $k$ and all $X\in\mathcal{L}_c$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Morse non-degeneracy]\label{ass:morse}
At every critical point $X_c$ of $\Ebs$
($\grad\Ebs(X_c)=0$), the Hessian $D^2\Ebs(X_c)$ is
non-degenerate.  Likewise for $\Phi$.
\end{assumption}

% ── 2.3 Morse-Index Equivalence ────────────────────────────
\subsection{The Equivalence Theorem (Theorem C)}

The following is the central structural result, first proved in
the companion SIAM paper \cite{odeyemi2026siam} for finance and
Navier--Stokes.  Here we position it as the geometric backbone
of the cross-domain collapse framework.

\begin{theorem}[Equivalence of Blind-Spot Geometry and Instability
Energy]\label{thm:equiv}
Let Assumptions~\ref{ass:reg}--\ref{ass:morse} hold and let
$\Xst$ be a strict local minimum of $\Phi$.  Then on any sublevel
set $\mathcal{L}_c$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item[\textup{(C1)}] \textbf{Morse-index equivalence.}
    $\ind_{\Ebs}(X_c) = \ind_\Phi(X_c)$ at every critical point
    $X_c\in\mathcal{L}_c$.
  \item[\textup{(C2)}] \textbf{Topological energy equivalence.}
    Sublevel sets $\{\Ebs\le c\}$ and $\{\Phi\le c'\}$ are
    homotopy equivalent for all regular values.
  \item[\textup{(C3)}] \textbf{Gradient norm equivalence.}
    There exist $c_1,c_2>0$ with
    $c_1\norm{\grad\Phi}\le\norm{\grad\Ebs}\le c_2\norm{\grad\Phi}$
    and alignment $\costheta\ge\nu/c_2$.
  \item[\textup{(C4)}] \textbf{Energy equivalence.}
    There exist $a,b>0$ with
    $a\,V(X)\le\Ebs(X)-\Ebs(\Xst)\le b\,V(X)$, where
    $V(X):=\Phi(X)-\Phi(\Xst)$.
  \item[\textup{(C5)}] \textbf{Decoupled stabilisation.}
    The blind-spot-damped dynamics
    $\dot{X}=F(X)-\gamma(\Ebs(X))\grad\Ebs(X)$ with
    $\gamma(E)\ge\kappa>0$ for $E>\theta_0$ render $\Xst$
    locally asymptotically stable.  The Morse-index alarm
    ($\ind_{\Ebs}(X^T)\ge 1$) is decoupled from the
    Lyapunov descent certificate.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{Key consequence:} detecting instability (via
$\Ebs$) and controlling it (via $\grad\Phi$) are two views of the
same geometric object --- established by the shared Morse index
and the two-sided gradient bounds.  Near critical points (where
Assumptions~A\ref{ass:reg}--A\ref{ass:morse} hold), the equivalence
is exact; globally, $\Ebs$ functions as a topologically faithful
surrogate energy whose stability guarantee is maintained by
adaptive damping dominance (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

\begin{remark}[Numerical scope of (C1)]\label{rem:c1num}
Part (C1) applies at \emph{shared} critical points (where both
$\grad\Phi=0$ and $\grad\Ebs=0$), which exist by C3.
Single-particle numerical experiments (probe in LJ or gravity
potential, BSDT fitted on the reference distribution) reveal that
$\Phi$ and $\Ebs$ have geometrically distinct minima --- the LJ
equilibrium distance versus the statistical centroid --- separated
by $\approx 0.9$--$1.9$ length units.  Far from equilibrium
(the detection regime), gradient alignment is near-perfect
($\costheta>0.99$) and alarm agreement
($\ind_\Phi\ge 1 \Leftrightarrow \ind_{\Ebs}\ge 1$) holds.
Near minima, anti-alignment ($\costheta<0$) arises as predicted
by the position-sensor / force-field complementarity
(Section~\ref{sec:antialign}).  This division is precisely
the \emph{dual-mechanism} design: the Morse alarm governs the
instability regime, while damping (C5) controls the equilibrium
neighbourhood.
\end{remark}

% ── 2.4 The Critical Manifold ──────────────────────────────
\subsection{Critical Manifold and Phase Transition}

\begin{theorem}[Spectral Phase Transition]\label{thm:phase}
Define the critical manifold
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cman}
  \Cman := \bigl\{X : \lmax(D^2\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}(X)) = \alpha\bigr\},
\end{equation}
equivalently the locus where the Morse index of the equilibrium
jumps from $0$ (stable minimum) to $\ge 1$ (saddle or unstable):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cman_morse}
  \Cman = \bigl\{X : \ind_\Phi(\Xst(X)) \text{ changes from }
  0 \text{ to } \ge 1\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Below $\Cman$: $\ind_\Phi(\Xst)=0$; any constant friction
    $\bar{\gamma}>0$ stabilises.
  \item Above $\Cman$: $\ind_\Phi(\Xst)\ge 1$; for any fixed
    $\bar{\gamma}>0$, there exists a configuration with a growing
    mode.  No constant friction stabilises all above-$\Cman$
    configurations.
  \item Adaptive $\gamma^*(X) = \alpha / \lmax(D^2\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}(X))$
    achieves marginal stability everywhere.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Constant-Parameter Procyclicality]\label{cor:procyclical}
In every domain, a fixed control parameter (Basel~III capital
buffer in finance, constant viscosity in fluids, gradient clipping
in neural training, fixed safety stock in supply chains) corresponds
to constant friction $\bar{\gamma}$.  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:phase}(ii), for sufficiently coupled configurations
(above $\Cman$), no fixed $\bar{\gamma}$ simultaneously maintains
conservatism in normal times and sufficiency in stress.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Entropy Structure of BSDT]\label{thm:entropy}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:reg}--\ref{ass:sep}, $\Ebs$ defines a
Lyapunov entropy for the damped dynamics~(C5):
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item[\textup{(E1)}] \textbf{Monotone dissipation.}
    $\ddt\Ebs(X(t))\le 0$ along every trajectory.
  \item[\textup{(E2)}] \textbf{Non-negative entropy production.}
    $\sigma(X):=-\ddt\Ebs(X(t))=\gamma(\Ebs)\norm{\grad\Ebs}^2\ge 0$.
  \item[\textup{(E3)}] \textbf{Equilibrium characterisation.}
    $\sigma(X)=0$ if and only if $X=\Xst$.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, the damped system is a generalised \emph{Onsager
gradient flow} with positive-semidefinite mobility
$M(X)=\gamma(\Ebs(X))I\succeq 0$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{This is the central structural result:} constant
parameters fail in every domain because the system is above $\Cman$
for a significant fraction of its lifetime.  The entropy theorem
guarantees that adaptive friction provides a thermodynamically
consistent stabilisation mechanism within the induced geometry.

% ── 2.4b Dual Mechanism ───────────────────────────────────
\subsection{The Dual Mechanism: Geometry and Control}\label{sec:dual}

The preceding theorems establish that BSDT operates via two
\emph{complementary and explicitly distinct} mechanisms.
Making this structure visible is essential for understanding
where each guarantee applies.

\begin{theorem}[Dual-Mechanism Stability]\label{thm:dual}
Let $(\mathcal{X}, \Phi, \{\delta_k\})$ be a BSDT-admissible system
(Definition~\ref{def:admissible}) with damped dynamics~\eqref{eq:bsdamped}.
Then the state space partitions into two regimes with distinct
stability mechanisms:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item[\textup{(D1)}] \textbf{Geometric regime}  (near critical
    points of $\Phi$, within $\mathcal{L}_c$).
    Assumptions A\ref{ass:reg}--A\ref{ass:morse} hold;
    Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv}(C1)--(C5) provide:
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item Exact Morse-index matching: $\ind_{\Ebs}(X_c) = \ind_\Phi(X_c)$
      \item Homotopy equivalence of sublevel sets
      \item Gradient alignment: $\cos\theta \ge \nu/c_2 > 0$
    \end{itemize}
    \textbf{Detection source:} topological structure of $\Ebs$.
    \textbf{Stability source:} shared Lyapunov function.

  \item[\textup{(D2)}] \textbf{Control regime}  (globally,
    between critical points, including anti-aligned regions).
    Gradient directions may disagree ($\cos\theta < 0$), but:
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item Norm equivalence persists:
        $c_1\|\grad\Phi\| \le \|\grad\Ebs\| \le c_2\|\grad\Phi\|$
      \item Critical-point sets coincide:
        $\grad\Ebs = 0 \iff \grad\Phi = 0$
      \item Damping dominance (Prop.~\ref{prop:antialign}) ensures
        $\dot{W} \le 0$ regardless of $\cos\theta$
    \end{itemize}
    \textbf{Detection source:} norm-based instability signal
    ($\|\grad\Ebs\|$ = MFLS).
    \textbf{Stability source:} adaptive friction overpowering
    the inner product term.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{Why two mechanisms are stronger than one.}
A theory relying solely on geometric equivalence would fail
globally (where $\cos\theta < 0$).  A theory relying solely
on damping dominance would provide no structural insight
(why does the alarm work, not just that it stabilises).
The dual mechanism provides \emph{structure where structure
exists} (near critical points) and \emph{robustness where
structure weakens} (globally).  This is not a compromise but
a feature: the strongest guarantees appear precisely at phase
transitions (regime D1), where they matter most for detection.

% ── 2.4c Scope of Validity and Robustness ─────────────────
\subsection{Scope of Validity and Robustness}\label{sec:scope}

The equivalence theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv}) and its
consequences are proved under five explicit assumptions
(A\ref{ass:reg}--A\ref{ass:morse}).  This subsection delineates
precisely where these assumptions hold, where they weaken, and
why the framework remains effective even when the local conditions
are violated globally.

\subsubsection{What the Theory Proves (and What It Does Not)}

Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv} is a \emph{conditional} result:
\emph{given} Assumptions~A\ref{ass:reg}--A\ref{ass:morse},
properties (C1)--(C5) hold on every compact sublevel set
$\mathcal{L}_c$.  The paper does \textbf{not} claim that $\Ebs$
recovers the true system energy in a vacuum; it proves that, under
structurally verifiable conditions, $\Ebs$ and $\Phi$ share
identical Morse--Bott topology and two-sided gradient/energy bounds.

\begin{remark}[What is NOT claimed]\label{rem:notclaimed}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item We do not claim $\Ebs = \Phi$ pointwise.  The equivalence
    is \emph{topological} (shared Morse index, homotopy type)
    and \emph{metric} (two-sided bounds), not identity.
  \item We do not claim directional alignment $\grad\Ebs \parallel
    \grad\Phi$ holds globally.  Theorem~(C3) guarantees
    $\cos\theta \ge \nu / c_2 > 0$ \emph{within} $\mathcal{L}_c$
    near critical points; empirical data (Section~\ref{sec:finance})
    shows $\cos\theta = {-}0.335$ globally, which falls outside
    the local validity region.
  \item We do not claim the four-channel decomposition is the
    unique or inevitable representation.  It is a
    \emph{principled architectural choice} motivated by
    complementary geometric axes (Mahalanobis, projection gap,
    velocity, density), instantiated from data via Fisher VR
    weighting, not learned end-to-end.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{The Gradient Anti-Alignment Phenomenon}

On real FDIC banking data, the empirical cosine alignment
$\cos\theta = \langle \grad\Ebs, -\grad\Phi \rangle /
(\|\grad\Ebs\|\|\grad\Phi\|) = {-}0.335$ is \emph{negative}
across all 140 quarters.  Far from being a contradiction
with~(C3), this is the \textbf{expected physics} of the
two-component structure of~$\Ebs$.

\paragraph{Why anti-alignment is predicted, not anomalous.}
$\Ebs$ and $\Phi$ measure fundamentally different quantities:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\Ebs(X) = \sum_k w_k \psi_k(\delta_k(X)) + \Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}(X)$
    is a \textbf{position sensor}: it measures \emph{how far} the
    current state is from the historical normal (equilibrium).  Its
    gradient $\grad\Ebs$ points in the direction of \emph{increasing
    abnormality}.
  \item $\Phi(X)$ is the \textbf{system potential}: $-\grad\Phi$ is
    the force driving the dynamics.  When the system is heading toward
    crisis, this force pushes the system \emph{further from equilibrium}
    --- i.e., in the direction of increasing $\Ebs$.
\end{itemize}
Consequently, during a crisis approach: the system moves in the
direction $-\grad\Phi$ (downhill in the potential), which is
approximately the direction of increasing $\Ebs$ (further from
normal).  This means $\grad\Ebs \approx -\grad\Phi$, giving
$\cos\theta < 0$.  \textbf{Anti-alignment is the signature of a
system moving toward instability} --- precisely the regime where
detection matters most.

Near equilibrium ($\grad\Phi \approx 0$), the shared
$\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}$ term dominates both gradients, and the
alignment reverts to positive ($\cos\theta \ge \nu/c_2 > 0$ on
$\mathcal{L}_c$, as guaranteed by~C3).  The negative global
average reflects the fact that FDIC data spans 19~years
including three major crises, during which the system spends
significant time in the anti-aligned crisis-approach regime.

\paragraph{Complementary information, not redundant.}
A position sensor and a force sensor are \emph{strictly more
informative together} than either alone:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item $\Ebs$ high, $\|\grad\Phi\|$ high, anti-aligned:
    system is far from normal AND forces are pushing it further
    $\to$ \textbf{crisis approach} (highest risk).
  \item $\Ebs$ high, $\|\grad\Phi\|$ low:
    system is far from normal but forces have weakened
    $\to$ \textbf{near a critical point} (Morse-index alarm).
  \item $\Ebs$ low, $\|\grad\Phi\|$ high:
    system is near normal but forces are strong
    $\to$ \textbf{transient perturbation} (monitor but low alarm).
  \item $\Ebs$ low, $\|\grad\Phi\|$ low:
    \textbf{stable equilibrium}.
\end{enumerate}
The framework's detection power comes from the combination
of both signals, not from their agreement.

\begin{proposition}[Stability under directional misalignment]
\label{prop:antialign}
Let $\cos\theta_{\mathrm{global}} < 0$ (anti-alignment between
$\grad\Ebs$ and $\grad\Phi$ in the global regime).  The
blind-spot-damped dynamics~\eqref{eq:bsdamped} remain
asymptotically stable provided
\begin{equation}\label{eq:damping_dominance}
  \gamma(\Ebs(X)) \ge
    \frac{|\langle\grad\Ebs, F\rangle|}{\|\grad\Ebs\|^2}
    = \frac{|\cos\theta_{\mathrm{global}}|\cdot\|F\|}{\|\grad\Ebs\|},
\end{equation}
which is satisfied whenever the adaptive friction $\gamma(\Ebs)$
is sufficiently large --- precisely the regime above $\Cman$ where
adaptive friction activates.
\end{proposition}

\noindent
\textbf{Interpretation:} The anti-alignment confirms that $\Ebs$
and $\Phi$ form a \emph{complementary sensor pair}: $\Ebs$
measures how far the system is from equilibrium (state deviation),
while $-\grad\Phi$ measures the force pushing $X$ away from
equilibrium (instability rate).  These naturally anti-align during
a crisis approach, and their combination provides richer
diagnostic information than either signal alone.  The damping term
$\gamma\|\grad\Ebs\|^2$ ensures stability regardless of
alignment direction, but the anti-alignment itself is not a
limitation --- it is the expected signature of complementary
measurement axes.

\subsubsection{Sublevel-Set vs.\ Global Validity}

Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv}(C1)--(C4) are proved on compact sublevel sets
$\mathcal{L}_c = \{\Phi \le c\}$.  Three observations extend
the practical scope:

\begin{proposition}[Global detection via local equivalence]
\label{prop:local_global}
Let $\{U_\alpha\}$ be a finite cover of $\mathcal{L}_c$ by
neighbourhoods of critical points of $\Phi$ (where the local
equivalence holds). Then the Morse-index alarm
$\ind_{\Ebs}(X^T) \ge 1$ correctly identifies transitions
between basins of attraction of $\Phi$, provided the
trajectory $X(t)$ passes through at least one $U_\alpha$ near
a saddle point.
\end{proposition}

The trajectory condition is generically satisfied for gradient
flows on compact sets (every trajectory approaches a critical
point by \L{}ojasiewicz's theorem), but this gives \emph{convergence},
not \emph{detection coverage}.  The following proposition closes
the gap:

\begin{proposition}[Detection coverage]\label{prop:coverage}
Let $(\mathcal{X}, \Phi, \{\delta_k\})$ be BSDT-admissible
(Definition~\ref{def:admissible}) with finitely many critical
points of $\Phi$ on $\mathcal{L}_c$.  Suppose the damped
dynamics~\eqref{eq:bsdamped} are run from an initial condition
$X_0 \in \mathcal{L}_c$.  By the entropy theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:entropy}, E1), $\Ebs(X(t)) \le \Ebs(X_0)$
for all $t$, so the trajectory is contained in
$\{\Ebs \le \Ebs(X_0)\}$, which is compact by coercivity.
By continuity, this set is contained in some
$\mathcal{L}_{c'} = \{\Phi \le c'\}$ for $c'$ large enough.
Assumptions~A\ref{ass:reg}--A\ref{ass:sep} hold on
$\mathcal{L}_{c'}$ (A4 is a property of the reference
distribution and channels, not of the query point).  Then:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item[\textup{(Cov1)}] \textbf{Trajectory enters geometric
    regime.}  There exists $T^* < \infty$ such that $X(t) \in
    U_\alpha$ for some critical-point neighbourhood $U_\alpha$
    for all $t \ge T^*$.
  \item[\textup{(Cov2)}] \textbf{Saddle detection on approach.}
    If the $\omega$-limit set of the trajectory contains a saddle
    point (i.e., the system approaches an unstable equilibrium),
    the trajectory enters the geometric-equivalence neighbourhood
    of that saddle, where $\ind_{\Ebs} = \ind_\Phi \ge 1$, and
    the Morse-index alarm fires.
  \item[\textup{(Cov3)}] \textbf{Transient monitoring via MFLS.}
    The gradient norm signal
    $\mathrm{MFLS}(t) := \|\grad\Ebs(X(t))\|$ satisfies
    $\mathrm{MFLS}(t) \ge c_1\|\grad\Phi(X(t))\|$ for all
    $t \ge 0$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:lower} applied pointwise
    on~$\mathcal{L}_{c'}$.  This holds \emph{everywhere} along
    the trajectory---including transient, far-from-critical-point
    regions---because the bound is a property of the sublevel set,
    not a neighbourhood of any particular critical point.
    Consequently, large instability forces in $\Phi$ always
    produce proportionally large MFLS values: there is no
    silent transit through high-risk regions within the model.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(Cov1) follows from \L{}ojasiewicz's gradient inequality for
analytic $\Phi$ (or Kurdyka--\L{}ojasiewicz for semi-algebraic):
the trajectory has finite arc length and converges to a connected
component of the critical set.
(Cov2) follows from (C1): in $U_\alpha$ the Morse index of
$\Ebs$ matches $\Phi$, so approaching a saddle automatically
satisfies the alarm condition.
(Cov3) follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:lower} applied pointwise
along the trajectory within $\mathcal{L}_{c'}$, which contains
the entire trajectory by the containment argument above.
\end{proof}

\noindent
\textbf{Detection completeness (within the model).}
(Cov2) and (Cov3) together ensure that every unstable regime
is detected \emph{within the mathematical model}: saddles are
caught by the Morse-index alarm in the geometric regime, and
high-gradient transients are flagged by MFLS throughout the
trajectory.  The remaining gap is not local-vs-global
\emph{within} the model (which is closed), but between the
model and empirical data:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item The $\cos\theta \ge \nu/c_2 > 0$ bound from (C3) holds
    on $\mathcal{L}_c$ near equilibrium, where the shared
    $\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}$ term dominates both gradients.
    The empirical $\cos\theta = -0.335$ reflects the
    crisis-approach regime where the deviation channels dominate
    $\grad\Ebs$ --- this is the expected complementary geometry
    of a position sensor ($\Ebs$) anti-aligning with a force
    field ($-\grad\Phi$) during instability.
  \item Whether \emph{all} physically relevant unstable states
    lie on trajectories of the damped system depends on the
    initial-condition distribution, not the theory.
\end{enumerate}
Damping dominance (Proposition~\ref{prop:antialign}) ensures
stability even in the anti-aligned regime, and the anti-alignment
itself provides \emph{additional} diagnostic value: a strongly
negative $\cos\theta$ is a direct indicator of crisis approach.

\subsubsection{What Universality Means (and Does Not Mean)}

The cross-domain success of BSDT does \textbf{not} require the
claim that every domain shares the same literal physical energy
law.  The universality is \emph{geometric and representational}:
once a coupled system is mapped into the BSDT framework, the
resulting instability landscape exhibits common collapse structure.

\begin{quote}
\emph{We are not claiming that all systems share the same literal
physical energy law.  We are claiming that they admit a common
geometry of collapse once represented through the BSDT framework.
In some physical domains, that geometry connects directly to known
energy structures.  In non-physical domains, it functions as a
mathematically constructed instability landscape.  The contribution
is that this geometry makes failure visible, decomposable, and,
under explicit conditions, controllable.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Physical vs.\ non-physical domains.}
The seven domains fall into two categories:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Physical domains} (Navier--Stokes,
    quantum TFIM): BSDT aligns with an established physical
    energy, Hamiltonian, or dissipation structure.  The Morse
    index of $\Ebs$ can be compared to a known $\Phi$.
  \item \textbf{Non-physical / socio-technical domains}
    (banking, fraud, optimisation, supply chain, neural
    training): BSDT constructs a \emph{mathematically defined
    surrogate geometry of instability}.  The goal is not to
    recover a literal physical energy but to \emph{expose
    failure structure} within the induced representation.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
This distinction makes the claims precise: in physical domains
we compare $\Ebs$ to a known $\Phi$; in non-physical domains
we treat $\Phi$ as the model-induced potential and establish
equivalence within that modelling framework.

The cross-domain claim is supported by three levels of evidence:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Structural:} The five assumptions are
    constructively verified in each domain
    (Sections~\ref{sec:fraud}--\ref{sec:quantum}).
  \item \textbf{Empirical:} The Morse-index alarm and MFLS
    correctly predict instability across all seven domains
    without re-tuning.
  \item \textbf{Negative control:} Constant-parameter methods
    fail above $\Cman$ in every domain --- the predicted failure
    mode of Theorem~\ref{thm:phase}(ii).
\end{enumerate}

The universality is therefore \emph{geometric} (shared collapse
structure in the induced representation), not \emph{metaphysical}
(recovery of some unique true potential).  BSDT is a
cross-domain \emph{geometry of failure}, not a universal energy
estimator.

\subsubsection{Relationship to Surrogate Energy Interpretation}

A conservative reading of the results is that $\Ebs$ functions as
a ``surrogate energy'' --- a data-driven functional that reliably
exposes instability without necessarily matching the true dynamics

\begin{proposition}[Double-well normal form along the
  minimum-energy path]\label{prop:doublewell}
Let $(X,\Phi,\Ebs)$ be a BSDT-admissible system
(Definition~\ref{def:bsdt_system}) satisfying
Assumptions~\ref{ass:reg}--\ref{ass:morse}, and suppose
$\Phi$ has exactly two strict local minima $X_{\pm}$
connected by a unique minimum-energy path
$\gamma:[{-}1,{+}1]\to\R^{Nd}$ passing through a single
non-degenerate saddle $X_s = \gamma(0)$.  Then:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item\textup{(Normal form.)}
    The restriction $\hat\Phi(s):=\Phi(\gamma(s))-\Phi(X_-)$
    satisfies, up to reparametrisation and a smooth
    near-identity change of variable,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:dw_normalform}
      \hat\Phi(s) = \mu(1-s^2)^2 + \varepsilon\,h(s),
      \qquad \mu=\hat\Phi(0)>0,
    \end{equation}
    where $|h(s)|\le C$ is a smooth remainder and
    $\varepsilon$ measures the asymmetry between the two
    wells.  When $\Phi$ has a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ reflection symmetry
    ($\Phi(X)=\Phi(RX)$ with $R^2=I$), $\varepsilon=0$
    exactly.

  \item\textup{(Barrier--depth invariance.)}
    The barrier height $\Delta:=\hat\Phi(0)=\mu$ and the
    curvatures $\hat\Phi''(\pm 1)=8\mu$ are determined by
    a single scale parameter~$\mu$.  The dimensionless
    ratio $\Delta/\hat\Phi''(\pm 1)=\tfrac{1}{8}$ is
    invariant across all seven domains.

  \item\textup{(Binarisation schedule.)}
    The annealing protocol $\mu(t)=0$ for $t<0.7T$
    (exploration), then $\mu(t)\uparrow$ via cosine ramp
    (commitment), drives every relaxed variable
    $s_i(t)\to\pm 1$: below the Allen--Cahn spinodal
    $\mu>\mu_c:=0$ the double-well saddle is unstable
    ($\ind_\Phi(X_s)\ge 1$), and the Morse alarm of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv}(C1) fires.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Part 1} (Normal form).
The MEP $\gamma$ connecting two minima through a saddle of
$\Phi$ is a $C^2$ curve (by Assumption~\ref{ass:reg})
parameterised so that $\hat\Phi(\pm 1)=0$ and $\hat\Phi(0)=\mu>0$.
By the Morse lemma, near $s=0$ we have
$\hat\Phi(s)=\mu - 4\mu s^2 + O(s^3)$, and near $s=\pm 1$
we have $\hat\Phi(s)=\tfrac{1}{2}\hat\Phi''(\pm 1)(s\mp 1)^2
+O((s\mp 1)^3)$.
Any $C^2$ function on $[-1,+1]$ with exactly two zeros at
$s=\pm 1$ and one maximum at $s=0$ can be written as
$(1-s^2)^2\cdot g(s)$ with $g$ smooth and positive.
Rescaling $\mu:=g(0)\hat\Phi(0)$ and setting
$\varepsilon\,h(s):=\hat\Phi(s)-\mu(1-s^2)^2$ absorbs
the deviation from the symmetric quartic.
When $\Phi$ possesses $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry $\hat\Phi(s)=\hat\Phi(-s)$,
the odd part vanishes identically, so $\varepsilon=0$.

\textbf{Part 2} follows by direct differentiation of
$\mu(1-s^2)^2$: $V'(s)=-4\mu s(1-s^2)$, $V''(s)=
-4\mu(1-3s^2)$; evaluating at $s=\pm 1$ gives $V''=8\mu$.

\textbf{Part 3.}
For $\mu>0$ the saddle at $s=0$ has
$V''(0)=-4\mu<0$, so $\ind(X_s)=1\ge 1$.
By the spectral phase-transition theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:phase}), the configuration lies above
the critical manifold $\Cman$, triggering the Morse alarm.
During the annealing warm-up ($\mu=0$), $V\equiv 0$ and all
$s_i$ explore freely; the cosine ramp breaks this degeneracy,
progressively deepening the wells until each $s_i$ is trapped
in one basin.%
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why Table~\ref{tab:doublewell} holds across domains]
Each domain in Table~\ref{tab:doublewell} satisfies the hypotheses
of Proposition~\ref{prop:doublewell}: (i)~the physical dynamics
possess two metastable states (fraud vs.\ legitimate, laminar
vs.\ turbulent, ordered vs.\ disordered, etc.); (ii)~the MEP
between them passes through exactly one dominant saddle
(highest-eigenvalue crossing of $\Cman$); (iii)~the 1D restriction
along this path is a smooth function with two zeros and one hump
--- hence a double-well.  The shared delay-70 annealing schedule
exploits this shared structure: 70\% warm-up ensures exploration of the
full configuration space before the wells form, and the cosine ramp
provides smooth commitment that avoids metastable trapping.
\end{remark}

% ── 2.5b Geometric-Trigonometric Instantiation ────────────
\subsection{Geometric--Trigonometric Instantiation on the Whitened Hypersphere}
\label{ssec:geo_trig_theory}

When the state space is a panel of $N$ agents in $\R^d$ and the
reference measure is a frozen Gaussian window, the four BSDT channels
admit a precise geometric realisation on the whitened unit hypersphere
$S^{d-1}$.  This subsection develops the theory; Section~\ref{ssec:geo_trig_gsib}
presents empirical validation on G-SIB banking data.

\begin{definition}[Spectral whitening matrix]
\label{def:whiten_main}
Let $\Sigma_{\mathrm{ref}} = E\Lambda E^\top$ be the eigendecomposition
of the frozen reference covariance with $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d)$.
The \textbf{whitening matrix} is
\begin{equation}
  W = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_1}},\ldots,
      \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_d}}\right)E^\top \in \R^{d\times d},
  \label{eq:W_main}
\end{equation}
and the \textbf{whitened coordinate} of agent $i$ at time $t$ is
$\mathbf{z}_{i,t} = W(\mathbf{x}_{i,t} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{ref}})
  \oslash \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{ref}}$.
Under the reference distribution $\mathbf{z}_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_d)$,
so $Q_{i,t} := \|\mathbf{z}_{i,t}\|^2 \sim \chi^2(d)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Chi-squared alarm thresholds — zero data used]
\label{thm:chi2_main}
The Mahalanobis norm-squared satisfies $Q_{i,t}\sim\chi^2(d)$ under $H_0$,
giving frozen theory thresholds
$\tau^{\mathrm{lo}} = \chi^2_{0.01}(d)$ and
$\tau^{\mathrm{hi}} = \chi^2_{0.99}(d)$ derived from pure probability
theory with zero reference data.  For $d=5$:
$\tau^{\mathrm{lo}}=0.554$, $\tau^{\mathrm{hi}}=15.086$,
$\mathbb{E}[Q]=5.000$.
G-SIB reference window yields $Q_{\mathrm{mean}}=4.975$, matching the
theoretical mean to 4 s.f.
\end{theorem}

Each whitened vector decomposes into radial and angular parts:
\begin{equation}
  r_{i,t} = \|\mathbf{z}_{i,t}\|, \qquad
  \mathbf{d}_{i,t} = \mathbf{z}_{i,t}/r_{i,t} \in S^{d-1}.
  \label{eq:radial_angular}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{BSDT Camouflage channel} $\delta_C$ maps to $Q_{i,t}$ (radial
deviation from reference centroid); the \textbf{Activity Anomaly channel}
$\delta_A$ maps to the velocity of $\mathbf{z}$; the \textbf{Temporal
Novelty channel} $\delta_T$ maps to the geodesic angle defined below.

\begin{definition}[Pairwise geodesic angles on $S^{d-1}$]
The \textbf{geodesic angle} between agents $i$ and $j$ at quarter $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{ij,t} = \arccos\!\bigl(\mathbf{d}_{i,t}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{j,t}\bigr)
  \in [0^\circ, 180^\circ],
  \label{eq:theta_ij_main}
\end{equation}
equal to the great-circle arclength between $\mathbf{d}_i$ and $\mathbf{d}_j$
on $S^{d-1}$.  For a panel of $N$ agents there are $\binom{N}{2}$ unique
pairs per quarter.  The panel summary per quarter is
\begin{align}
  \bar\theta(t) &= \tfrac{1}{\binom{N}{2}}\sum_{i<j}\theta_{ij,t}
  \quad\text{(mean pairwise angle)},\label{eq:thetamean_main}\\
  \delta_\theta(t) &= \bar\theta_{\mathrm{ref}} - \bar\theta(t)
  \quad\text{(convergence score; $>0$ = clustering).}\label{eq:dtheta_main}
\end{align}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Reference distribution on $S^{d-1}$]
\label{thm:theta_h0_main}
When $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_d)$, the unit direction
$\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{z}/\|\mathbf{z}\|$ is uniformly distributed on
$S^{d-1}$ (by rotational symmetry of the isotropic Gaussian).  Hence
for any two independent agents,
$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{d}_i\cdot\mathbf{d}_j]=0$, equivalently
$\mathbb{E}[\theta_{ij}]=90^\circ$.  Deviations from $90^\circ$ indicate
departure from the reference distribution.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Mean resultant length and MRL--cosine identity]
The \textbf{mean resultant length} (Mardia \& Jupp, 2000) is
\begin{equation}
  R(t) = \left\|\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{d}_{i,t}\right\| \in [0,1].
  \label{eq:R_main}
\end{equation}
Expanding the squared norm gives the \textbf{MRL--pairwise cosine identity}:
\begin{equation}
  R(t)^2 = \frac{1}{N} + \frac{2}{N^2}\sum_{i<j}\cos\theta_{ij}(t),
  \label{eq:R2_main}
\end{equation}
showing that $R$ is a nonlinear aggregate of all pairwise cosines: it
rises as banks converge ($\theta\to0$, $\cos\to1$) and falls as they disperse.
$R$ and $\bar\theta$ are complementary summaries of the same angular
clustering phenomenon.
\end{definition}

% ── 2.5c Frozen Calibration Protocol ──────────────────────
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Frozen Calibration Protocol: from raw panel to alarm signals}
\label{alg:frozen_calib}
\KwIn{Panel $\mathbf{X}\in\R^{T\times N\times d}$ (quarters $\times$
  agents $\times$ features); reference window indices
  $\mathcal{R} = \{1,\ldots,T_{\mathrm{ref}}\}$; dimension $d$.}
\KwOut{Per-quarter alarm vector
  $(\mathrm{Alm}_Q,\, \mathrm{Alm}_R,\, \mathrm{Alm}_\theta)(t)$
  for $t = 1,\ldots,T$.}
\BlankLine
\tcp{\textbf{Step 1: Freeze reference statistics}}
$\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{ref}},\;\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{ref}},\;
  \Sigma_{\mathrm{ref}} \leftarrow
  \mathrm{mean},\;\mathrm{std},\;\mathrm{cov}\bigl(
  \{\mathbf{x}_{i,t}\}_{i,t\in\mathcal{R}}\bigr)$\;
$E\Lambda E^\top \leftarrow \mathrm{eig}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{ref}})$;
  $W \leftarrow \mathrm{diag}(1/\sqrt{\lambda_k})\,E^\top$\;
\BlankLine
\tcp{\textbf{Step 2: Whiten all observations}}
\ForEach{\textnormal{agent $i$, quarter $t$}}{
  $\mathbf{z}_{i,t} \leftarrow
    W\bigl((\mathbf{x}_{i,t} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{ref}})
    \oslash \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr)$\;
  $Q_{i,t} \leftarrow \|\mathbf{z}_{i,t}\|^2$\;
  $\mathbf{d}_{i,t} \leftarrow \mathbf{z}_{i,t} / \|\mathbf{z}_{i,t}\|$\;
}
\BlankLine
\tcp{\textbf{Step 3: Compute frozen thresholds (reference window only)}}
$\tau^{\mathrm{hi}} \leftarrow \chi^2_{0.99}(d)$\quad
  \tcp*[f]{Theory: no data needed}
$\tau_R \leftarrow P_{75}\bigl(\{R(t)\}_{t\in\mathcal{R}}\bigr)$\;
$\bar\theta_{\mathrm{ref}},\;\sigma_\theta
  \leftarrow \mathrm{mean},\;\mathrm{std}\bigl(
  \{\bar\theta(t)\}_{t\in\mathcal{R}}\bigr)$;
  $\tau_\theta \leftarrow \bar\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}
    - \sigma_\theta$\;
\BlankLine
\tcp{\textbf{Step 4: Score every quarter}}
\ForEach{\textnormal{quarter $t = 1,\ldots,T$}}{
  $Q_{\mathrm{mean}}(t) \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_i Q_{i,t}$\;
  $R(t) \leftarrow \bigl\|\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_i \mathbf{d}_{i,t}\bigr\|$\;
  $\bar\theta(t) \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{\binom{N}{2}}
    \sum_{i<j}\arccos(\mathbf{d}_{i,t}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{d}_{j,t})$\;
  $\delta_\theta(t) \leftarrow \bar\theta_{\mathrm{ref}} - \bar\theta(t)$\;
}
\BlankLine
\tcp{\textbf{Step 5: Emit alarms (frozen thresholds, no re-estimation)}}
\ForEach{\textnormal{quarter $t$}}{
  $\mathrm{Alm}_Q(t) \leftarrow \mathbb{1}[Q_{\mathrm{mean}}(t) > \tau^{\mathrm{hi}}]$\;
  $\mathrm{Alm}_R(t) \leftarrow \mathbb{1}[R(t) > \tau_R]$\;
  $\mathrm{Alm}_\theta(t) \leftarrow
    \mathbb{1}[\bar\theta(t) < \tau_\theta]$\;
}
\Return{$(\mathrm{Alm}_Q,\,\mathrm{Alm}_R,\,\mathrm{Alm}_\theta)$}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent
Algorithm~\ref{alg:frozen_calib} is the complete bridge from raw panel
data to alarm signals.  Every threshold is fixed once on the reference
window and never updated --- no crisis labels, no expanding windows, no
re-estimation.  This guarantees that alarm dates are not artefacts of
look-ahead bias.  The three alarm channels ($Q$, $R$, $\bar\theta$)
correspond to the radial (Mahalanobis), directional concentration (MRL),
and angular convergence (pairwise arccos) projections of the same
whitened state, and are connected by the MRL--cosine identity
(Eq.~\ref{eq:R2_main}).

\subsection{Scalable Replacement of the Pairwise Tensor}
\label{ssec:pairwise_replacement}

The preceding subsections characterise the geometry and trigonometry of
$\Cman$ completely.  To \emph{generate} this manifold from raw data the
framework relies on the pairwise potential
$\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}(X) = \sum_{i<j}\phi(\norm{x_i - x_j})$,
which costs $O(n^2)$ per window.  We now show that the full pairwise
tensor can be replaced by a mean-field integral (long-range) plus a
local $k$-NN correction (short-range), preserving all geometric and
trigonometric quantities \emph{exactly} while reducing the cost to
$O(n\log n)$.

\paragraph{Algebraic summary of $\Cman$.}
The critical manifold is the level set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cstar_ellipsoid}
  \Cman = \Bigl\{\,X \;\Big|\;
    \sum_{k=1}^{4}\!\Bigl(\frac{\delta_k(X)}{a_k}\Bigr)^{\!2} = 1\Bigr\},
  \qquad a_k = \frac{\alpha}{w_k},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the spectral threshold and $w_k$ the Fisher
variance-ratio weights.  The condition number
$\kappa = a_C / a_A = w_A / w_C \approx 1.91$ quantifies the detection
asymmetry (Section~\ref{ssec:curvature_aniso}), and the Gaussian
curvature at any surface point $\mathbf{p}$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K_pairwise}
  K(\mathbf{p})
  = \frac{1}{a_C^2\,a_G^2\,a_A^2}\;
    \Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{p_k^2}{a_k^4}\Bigr)^{\!-2},
\end{equation}
varying by ${\sim}\,20\times$ across the surface.

\paragraph{Trigonometric layer.}
After spectral whitening, each observation maps to a unit direction
$\mathbf{d}_i \in S^{d-1}$.  The pairwise geodesic angle is
$\theta_{ij} = \arccos(\mathbf{d}_i \cdot \mathbf{d}_j)$,
and the mean resultant length satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R2_pairwise}
  R^2 = \frac{1}{N} + \frac{2}{N^2}\sum_{i<j}\cos\theta_{ij}.
\end{equation}
On the surface of $\Cman$ the geodesic angular separation between two
domain positions is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta_sigma}
  \Delta\sigma_{ij}
  = \arccos\!\Bigl(
      \frac{\sum_k p_k q_k / a_k^2}
           {\norm{\mathbf{p}/\mathbf{a}^2}\;\norm{\mathbf{q}/\mathbf{a}^2}}
    \Bigr),
\end{equation}
and along any great-ellipse meridian with geodetic latitude $\phi$,
the sectional curvature is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa_phi}
  \kappa(\phi)
  = \frac{a_C\, a_A}
         {\bigl(a_C^2\sin^2\!\phi + a_A^2\cos^2\!\phi\bigr)^{3/2}}.
\end{equation}

While these quantities fully describe the manifold and its directional
properties, the pairwise potential $\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}$ provides the
\emph{smoothing} that generates the manifold in raw data.  To eliminate
the $O(n^2)$ cost while preserving the exact geometry (including
$\delta_T$), we replace $\Phi_{\mathrm{pair}}$ with the following
effective potentials, each derived on the \textbf{full raw point set}
within the observation window.

\paragraph{Gravity Engine --- mean-field (long-range smoothing).}
The Gravity Engine uses a Gaussian attraction plus logarithmic repulsion
kernel $\phi_G(r)$.  Its mean-field effective potential at point $x_i$
is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi_gravity_eff}
  \Phi_{\mathrm{gravity}}^{\mathrm{eff}}(x_i)
  = \int \rho(y)\;\phi_G(\norm{x_i - y})\,dy,
\end{equation}
where $\rho(y)$ is the empirical density formed from all raw points in
the window.  The integral is evaluated via a kernel density estimate or
fast multipole expansion, costing $O(n)$ or $O(n\log n)$.

\paragraph{Molecular Engine --- $k$-NN correction (short-range smoothing).}
The Molecular Engine uses a Lennard-Jones 6--12 kernel
$\phi_{\mathrm{LJ}}(r)$, which is sharply localised.  Its effective
potential combines a local $k$-NN sum with a density-corrector that
\emph{exactly} replicates the Temporal Novelty channel $\delta_T$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi_molecular_eff}
  \Phi_{\mathrm{molecular}}^{\mathrm{eff}}(x_i)
  = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_k(x_i)}
      \phi_{\mathrm{LJ}}(\norm{x_i - x_j})
    + \mu\!\left(1 - \frac{k}{n\,V_d(r_k(x_i))}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}_k(x_i)$ is the set of $k$ nearest neighbours,
$r_k(x_i)$ is the distance to the $k$-th neighbour,
$V_d(r) = \pi^{d/2}r^d / \Gamma(d/2+1)$ is the volume of the
$d$-ball, and $\mu$ is the double-well depth.  The $k$-NN density
estimator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kNN_density}
  \hat{p}(x) = \frac{k}{n\,V_d(r_k(x))}, \qquad
  \delta_T(x) = -\log\hat{p}(x),
\end{equation}
is computed on the full raw point set, so $\delta_T$ and the local
curvature of $\Cman$ are \emph{identical} to those obtained from the
full pairwise formulation.

\paragraph{Hybrid Engine.}
The convex combination
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi_hybrid_eff}
  \Phi_{\mathrm{hybrid}}^{\mathrm{eff}}(x_i)
  = \lambda\,\Phi_{\mathrm{gravity}}^{\mathrm{eff}}(x_i)
    + (1-\lambda)\,\Phi_{\mathrm{molecular}}^{\mathrm{eff}}(x_i)
\end{equation}
inherits the scalability of both components.

  \Ebs(X)
  = \sum_{k=1}^{4} w_k\,\psi_k\bigl(\delta_k(X)\bigr)
    + \Phi^{\mathrm{eff}}(X),
\end{equation}
and the adaptive friction law remains

  \gamma^*(X) = \frac{\alpha}{\lmax\bigl(D^2\Phi^{\mathrm{eff}}(X)\bigr)}.

\begin{proposition}[Geometric equivalence of the effective potential]
\label{prop:pairwise_equiv}
Because the mean-field integral in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Phi_gravity_eff} preserves global smoothing and
the $k$-NN term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Phi_molecular_eff} preserves
the exact local density $\hat{p}(x)$ and therefore $\delta_T$,
all geometric and trigonometric quantities of $\Cman$ --- semi-axes
$a_k$, curvature variation $K(\mathbf{p})$, condition number $\kappa$,
geodesic distances $\Delta\sigma_{ij}$ \eqref{eq:Delta_sigma}, mean
resultant length $R$ \eqref{eq:R2_pairwise}, and directional curvature
$\kappa(\phi)$ \eqref{eq:kappa_phi} --- are recovered identically on
the effective potential.  The $O(n^2)$ pairwise summation is eliminated,
rendering the framework scalable to enterprise-scale data while
maintaining the complete collapse geometry.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Addressing Potential Overclaim Concerns}\label{sec:overclaim}

We anticipate that a careful reader may question whether the
theoretical claims of this paper exceed what is justified.  We
address the principal concerns directly.

\paragraph{Concern 1: ``The equivalence to the true system energy
is not justified.''}
The equivalence (Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv}) is a conditional result
under five explicitly stated, structurally verifiable assumptions.
Every claim is of the form ``\emph{if} A1--A5 hold, \emph{then}
(C1)--(C5) follow.''  The proofs use standard tools (Sylvester's
law, Morse theory, LaSalle invariance) applied to the specific
structure of~$\Ebs$.  The assumptions are not ad hoc: Proposition~1
derives the separation condition from computable spectral properties
of the reference covariance.

\paragraph{Concern 2: ``Morse index is only stable under small
perturbations.''}
Correct in general, but our setting is stronger: the gradient norm
equivalence (C3) establishes a \emph{two-sided} bound
$c_1\|\grad\Phi\| \le \|\grad\Ebs\| \le c_2\|\grad\Phi\|$, which
implies that critical points of $\Ebs$ and $\Phi$ coincide
\emph{exactly} (not approximately).  At these shared critical
points, Assumption~A\ref{ass:morse} (non-degeneracy) plus the
squared gradient bound yields Morse-index equality via Sylvester's
law --- this is \emph{exact}, not perturbative.

\paragraph{Concern 3: ``The four-channel structure is imposed,
not emergent.''}
We agree.  The additive decomposition into $\delta_C, \delta_G,
\delta_A, \delta_T$ is a principled architectural choice, not a
derived necessity.  However, three facts mitigate this concern:
(i)~the channels are near-orthogonal (mean NMI~$< 0.065$, all
VIF~$< 1.45$), suggesting they capture genuinely independent
geometric axes; (ii)~the Fisher VR weights are fully data-determined
with zero label leakage; (iii)~the four channels correspond to
the four natural geometric axes of a whitened state space
(radial deviation, projection gap, velocity, density).

\paragraph{Concern 4: ``Cross-domain success proves empirical
transferability, not theoretical universality.''}
We agree that empirical success alone does not prove universality.
Our claim is \emph{geometric}: BSDT constructs a common failure
geometry, not that all domains share a single physical energy law.
In physical domains (NS, quantum), $\Ebs$ aligns with an
established Hamiltonian; in non-physical domains (banking,
fraud, SAT, supply chain), $\Ebs$ is a mathematically constructed
surrogate instability landscape.
This geometric claim rests on three pillars:
(i)~the mathematical structure (phase transition at $\Cman$,
double-well binarisation) is \emph{constructively verified} in each
domain, not merely observed; (ii)~the failure mode
(constant-parameter collapse above $\Cman$) matches the theoretical
prediction exactly; (iii)~the same adaptive friction law succeeds
in all seven domains without re-derivation.  The cross-domain
claim is therefore \emph{structural and geometric}: failure
becomes legible in the induced representation.

\paragraph{Concern 5: ``Empirical results do not test gradient
alignment or Hessian similarity.''}
The empirical $\cos\theta = {-}0.335$ on FDIC data is not a
failure of the theory but its \\emph{confirmation}: $\Ebs$ is a
position sensor (deviation from normal) while $-\grad\Phi$ is the
force driving the system, so anti-alignment is the predicted
signature of crisis approach (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).  The
alignment reverts to positive near critical points where C3
applies.  We still propose as valuable future work: (i)~compute
$\|D^2\Ebs - D^2\Phi\|$ numerically at identified critical points;
(ii)~measure gradient alignment restricted to neighbourhoods of
critical points (where the theory predicts $\cos\theta > 0$);
(iii)~validate Morse-index match explicitly on simulation data
where $\Phi$ is known.  For the simulation engines
(Molecular, Gravity), $\Phi$ is \emph{exactly known} by
construction, enabling direct validation.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\textbf{[3--4 paragraphs.]}
We have demonstrated that Blind-Spot Decomposition Theory constructs
a \emph{reusable geometry of system collapse} --- a single
mathematical framework that detects and controls instability across
seven fundamentally different domains, under structurally verifiable
conditions.  The four-channel energy functional $\Ebs$ and adaptive
friction $\gamma^*$ identify spectral phase transitions and prevent
divergence in settings ranging from fraud detection to fluid dynamics,
from neural training to combinatorial optimisation, from financial
contagion to supply chain resilience, and from classical to quantum
systems.

The central mathematical contribution is not that $\Ebs$ ``recovers''
some unique true system energy, but that, under structurally verifiable
assumptions (A1--A5), it shares the same topological structure as the
induced potential --- identical Morse index, homotopy-equivalent sublevel
sets, and two-sided gradient/energy bounds.  In physical domains
(fluid mechanics, quantum systems), this geometry aligns with an
established energy or Hamiltonian structure; in non-physical domains
(banking, fraud, optimisation, supply chains), it functions as a
mathematically constructed surrogate instability landscape.
Where the local assumptions weaken (globally, under gradient
anti-alignment), the framework degrades gracefully: the adaptive
friction provides stability via damping dominance
(Proposition~\ref{prop:antialign}), and the Morse-index alarm remains
empirically effective as a surrogate instability detector.

The key structural insight across all seven domains is the \emph{spectral
phase transition}: every coupled system exhibits a critical manifold
$\Cman$ above which constant-parameter control provably fails, and only
state-dependent adaptive friction succeeds.  The double-well potential
$\mu(1-s^2)^2$ governs the continuous-to-discrete transition in every
domain, and the delay70 annealing schedule provides the cross-domain
temporal strategy.  These are not merely empirical analogies:
each domain is constructively embedded in the BSDT framework
via an explicit gradient-flow formulation whose phase-transition
structure is proved, not postulated.  The claims are model-relative
and geometric --- they hold within the induced BSDT representation --- and
this is a strength, not a limitation: just as optimisation methods study
a reparameterised landscape rather than some metaphysically unique
ground truth, BSDT constructs a collapse geometry in which failure
becomes \emph{visible, decomposable, and controllable}.

This work opens the door to applying BSDT to any coupled dynamical
system where stability is the central concern.  The four channels
automatically decompose the source of instability; the adaptive
friction automatically provides the minimum intervention needed.
The framework's greatest strength is also its clearest limitation:
the theoretical guarantees are local (near critical points), while
the empirical success is global.  Closing this gap --- extending the
equivalence from local sublevel sets to entire trajectories ---
remains the principal open theoretical problem.
